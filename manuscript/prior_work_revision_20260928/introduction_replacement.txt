Digital tools already support substantial parts of this task. Computer-aided synthesis planning proposes routes,[[25-27]] process simulation evaluates specified configurations,[[28,29]] and Bayesian or active-learning strategies guide experimental optimization.[[30-34]] AI-informed robotic flow synthesis has connected planning to physical execution.[[35]] RoboChem-Flex and UV-visible auto-reactometry further demonstrate modular reaction optimization and LLM-assisted photochemical screening, respectively.[[36,37]] These advances establish a strong foundation for automated experimental development. The question addressed here concerns a complementary task: assembling an inspectable initial flow-process proposal from a reaction procedure, the chemist's objectives, and the equipment available in a particular laboratory.

LLM tools and scientific agents provide routes from unstructured descriptions to coordinated actions. ChemCrow and Coscientist connect chemical reasoning with tools and experimentation, while El Agente orchestrates computational chemistry.[[38-40]] Recent Co-Scientist and Paper2Agent studies extend collaborative scientific reasoning and tested reuse of computational methods.[[43,47]] Procedural systems are particularly relevant: ACRA maps literature procedures to executable chemical descriptions and available hardware, AutoLabs combines clarification, calculation and self-correction for liquid handling, and PRISM introduces simulation feedback before robotic execution.[[45,46,48]] Together, these systems establish language-based interfaces, agent collaboration and hardware-aware validation as practical foundations for more integrated chemical workflows.

Direct precedents in continuous-flow chemistry sharpen the comparison. Pan et al.'s Chat-microreactor extracts literature-based flow information, predicts flow patterns, and provides knowledge-enhanced microreactor design guidance, including a piperacillin example.[[60]] Wang et al.'s SapoMind recommends and experimentally optimizes continuous lanolin saponification conditions.[[62]] Ruan et al.'s LLM-RDF has broader scope: its agents support synthesis development through experimental execution, kinetic analysis, optimization, purification, and reactor scale-up design.[[61]] These contributions extend beyond condition optimization to include reactor design and experimentally supported development. Their breadth shifts the comparison from whether a system uses agents or performs calculations to how it connects a reaction procedure, a process configuration and an available laboratory.

Related process-design work already links language to quantitative engineering representations. Tian et al. generate process topologies and configure parameters for executable simulations, evaluating the resulting flowsheets computationally.[[63]] The CeProAgents preprint organizes chemical process development into knowledge, concept and parameter cohorts, including flowsheet tasks and simulator-based optimization.[[64]] The CAAF preprint formalizes deterministic assertions and tests constraint conflicts in a constructed pharmaceutical flow-reactor design problem.[[65]] From the Aspuru-Guzik group, the El Agente Grafico and La Agente Optima preprints investigate typed execution state and agent-supervised optimization, including a physical flow-chemistry campaign in the latter.[[49,50]] The emerging landscape therefore spans chemical procedures, local hydrodynamics, process simulation and constraint validation, with different input-output representations and experimental boundaries.

FlowPilot focuses on translating reaction-level instructions into a connected, laboratory-specific flow configuration. This requires relating the order of chemical transformations to stream introduction, reagent concentrations and molar feeds, reactor volumes, stage-specific residence-time conventions, and compatible equipment assignments. FlowPilot addresses this integration task by jointly constructing and reconciling these quantities in one inspectable process record. This task differs in emphasis from predicting a local flow pattern, optimizing conditions on an established platform, or configuring a general process simulator, while overlapping with parts of each. The task-level comparison and its evidence boundaries are provided in ESI Section 11; it is not a performance ranking of the published systems.

Evaluation must distinguish numerical consistency from chemical performance. ChemBench demonstrates that strong aggregate chemical knowledge can coexist with elementary errors and overconfidence, while uncertainty-calibrated optimization research documents invalid suggestions from direct LLM prompting within defined reaction search spaces.[[51,52]] In a flow proposal, valid formatting or agreement among agents cannot establish correct stoichiometry, compatible hardware, or adequate reaction time. We therefore ask whether a workflow that separates chemical interpretation, deterministic calculation, and specialist review produces more consistent and complete designs than one-shot generation under the same chemistry and inventory information. The comparison tests the delivered workflows, not equal-compute performance or the isolated effect of agent count.

Here we introduce FlowPilot as a hybrid design workflow that connects standardized chemist intake, literature-grounded reasoning, quantitative engineering analysis, and inventory reconciliation. Intake records protocol facts, objectives, measured evidence, equipment limits and hypotheses separately. Chemistry interpretation and plan-aware retrieval supply relevant precedents; deterministic routines calculate dependent quantities; and a seven-agent council reviews and revises candidate operating choices. A common final record links stream tables, stage conditions, equipment assignments and the process diagram. The output is a quantitatively specified proposal for experimental implementation after laboratory review, not an automatically validated operating procedure. In particular, numerical closure cannot substitute for kinetic evidence, and a shorter residence time is a hypothesis to test rather than proof of successful intensification.

The study separates information support, computational design quality and prospective experimental performance. Corpus and retrieval analyses evaluate the context supplied to the generator. Matched architecture comparisons use a fixed rubric, deterministic verification evidence and separate LLM judges; module ablations and a historical isoxazole study examine configuration sensitivity.[[53]] Two connected-stage cases then define the laboratory evaluation: photoredox Giese addition followed by aerobic oxidation tests the translation of oxygen-free and oxygen-fed stages into one equipment configuration (Figure 5), while DPDTC-mediated amidation tests staged reagent addition, coupled flow calculations and intermediate transfer without isolation (Figure 6).[[54,55]] The experiments will assess whether the proposed configurations can be implemented and deliver useful chemical outcomes; the computational benchmark alone cannot establish either result.
