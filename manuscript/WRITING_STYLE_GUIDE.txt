Purpose

Use this paper as the stylistic reference for subsequent FlowPilot manuscript
and ESI revisions. Adapt its reasoning, continuity and measured scientific
voice to the user's chemistry and computational results. Write original prose;
do not transplant distinctive wording, physics content or unsupported claims.
The user's later instructions take precedence over this guide.

What makes the reference work

The introduction moves from the scientific objective to the specific limitation
that prevents further progress. Existing improvements are acknowledged before
their remaining limitation is identified. The proposed apparatus then follows
as a reasoned response to that limitation. Novelty is defined by the unresolved
question it addresses, rather than by a list of components or features.

The results develop a chain of evidence. Preparing and monitoring the reservoir
makes the transport test interpretable; the transport observations motivate
assessment of storage and vacuum performance; established reservoir stability
then provides the basis for investigating controlled extraction and merging.
Each stage advances the scientific argument and prepares the next stage.

Numerical evidence is interpreted against a meaningful reference. For example,
the observed change in the particle signal is compared with the calibrated
change expected from losing one particle before the authors infer preservation
of the reservoir. The number, comparison and inference belong to one argument.

The conclusion separates what has been demonstrated from transfers, upgrades
and longer-distance operation that remain to be implemented. Qualifications
occur near the observations or inferences they constrain. The Methods explain
why a procedure, calibration or correction is required before developing its
details. These are the patterns to carry into FlowPilot.

Narrative and paragraph construction

1. Begin a section with its scientific purpose, constraint or unresolved question.
   Establish why the reader needs the method or experiment before explaining it.
   For comparisons, make clear what uncertainty the comparison can resolve.

2. Give each paragraph one main task. A useful underlying progression is
   motivation, approach, observation and implication, but distribute these
   across sentences or paragraphs as the argument requires. Do not force every
   paragraph into the same four-sentence template.

3. Connect paragraphs through their scientific content. Carry forward a finding,
   limitation, requirement or defined object from the previous paragraph, then
   develop its consequence. Generic connectors cannot repair a missing logical
   relationship. Avoid a sequence of unrelated module descriptions.

4. Place familiar information before the new information it makes interpretable.
   Use concrete subjects and explicit causal relationships. Resolve ambiguous
   references such as 'this', 'these results' or 'the approach' when several
   possible antecedents exist. Repeat a necessary technical term rather than
   changing terminology merely for variety.

5. End paragraphs with the supported implication, the remaining question or the
   capability now established. Let that ending prepare the next paragraph.
   Avoid decorative concluding sentences and repeated claims of importance.

Sentences and scientific voice

Use direct, precise verbs. Use 'we' naturally for author actions and observations
when consistent with the manuscript. Vary sentence length: use longer sentences
for connected reasoning and shorter sentences to make a result or inference
clear. Avoid strings of subordinate clauses, inflated adjectives, promotional
language, artificial suspense and claims that something is essential without
explaining the dependency. Do not imitate every expression in the reference;
the target is its disciplined reasoning and continuity.

Additional user preferences from the ablation-section revision

Avoid colons in manuscript prose. Avoid announcing that a topic 'must be
considered', followed by a list of results or references. Integrate the reason
for discussing that topic into the developing argument. Do not refer to 'panel
a', 'panel b' or similar labels in running prose; attach conventional figure
citations to the relevant scientific statements instead. Explain scoring in
language a chemistry reader can understand before introducing aggregation
details. Interpret numerical comparisons in relation to the study question.
Explain what concrete design defects mean chemically and operationally, and
keep necessary qualifications concise and attached to the claims they affect.
Avoid sentences whose sole purpose is to announce what a table or figure
contains. Establish the relevant method or finding, then cite its supporting
figure or table.

Evidence and figures

Introduce a test through its purpose, then explain enough of the comparison and
measurement for the result to be understood. Report the relevant numerical
result and interpret its meaning without extending beyond the measured outcome.
Preserve sample sizes, units, baselines, uncertainty and study scope when they
matter to the inference. Distinguish observations from proposed explanations.
Use confident language for established findings and specific qualifications for
uncertain ones; neither blanket hedging nor blanket certainty is appropriate.

Embed figure citations where they support an argument. Do not structure results
paragraphs around openings such as 'Figure 3c shows' or 'Figure 3d illustrates'.
The subject of the paragraph should be the scientific question or finding.
Captions can explain panels systematically, including definitions, conditions
and uncertainty; the manuscript should explain why the evidence matters.

Main text, ESI and delivery

Keep the main text focused on rationale, decisive evidence and interpretation.
Include implementation details when they are needed to understand or assess a
claim. Use the ESI for reproducible definitions, procedures, scoring rules,
controls and sensitivity analyses, while retaining clear purpose and logical
sequence. Smooth writing must not conceal missing validation, negative findings
or a distinction between an offline diagnostic and the operational system.

For future user-supplied passages, first identify the passage's role in the
surrounding argument and preserve its factual content. Improve ordering and
connections, not just vocabulary. Do not add results, claims of novelty,
comparisons or causal explanations that the supplied evidence does not support.
Normally return the revised passage itself, without an editorial report or an
unsolicited list of alternatives. Add a brief separate note only when a material
scientific ambiguity prevents an accurate revision.

Final editorial check

Can the reader explain why this work or test was needed? Does each paragraph
follow from the previous one? Are the method, evidence and inference connected?
Do the figures support the narrative without becoming its subjects? Are claims
proportionate to the evidence? Is every retained technical detail useful? Does
the passage read as a scientific argument rather than a progress report?
