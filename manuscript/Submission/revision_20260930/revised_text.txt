INTRODUCTION

Continuous-flow chemistry offers a versatile platform for synthesis by controlling how reagents are mixed, heated, irradiated, and brought into contact with gases or catalysts.[[1-4]] These capabilities have expanded access to photochemical, electrochemical, and gas-liquid transformations and enabled practical routes to pharmaceutical intermediates.[[5-10]] Connecting reaction stages can further reduce intermediate handling and support multistep or on-demand production.[[11-14]] Realizing these advantages, however, requires more than transferring a batch recipe into a smaller vessel. A successful translation must connect the chemistry to a suitable reactor arrangement, quantitative feed conditions, and the equipment available in the laboratory.[[15,16]]

Those choices are closely coupled. Reactor geometry influences heat and mass transfer, irradiation, and pressure loss, while an interstage addition changes both composition and the flow entering the next reactor.[[17-21]] Gas-liquid processes introduce a further distinction between gas molar delivery and its pressure-dependent volume, making the definition of residence time particularly important.[[22]] In a telescoped sequence, the composition leaving one stage also determines what the next stage can achieve. Integrated apixaban synthesis and photochemical routes to sartan intermediates illustrate the coordination of chemistry, hardware, and operating conditions required in multistep flow development.[[23,24]]

Digital methods increasingly support this development process. Computer-aided synthesis planning identifies reaction routes,[[25-27]] process simulation evaluates connected unit operations,[[28,29]] and Bayesian and active-learning methods guide experimental optimization.[[30-34]] AI-informed robotic flow synthesis has linked planning to physical execution,[[35]] while RoboChem-Flex and UV-visible auto-reactometry extend modular optimization and LLM-assisted photochemical screening.[[36,37]] These approaches demonstrate the value of integrating computation with experimentation. Preparing an initial configuration from a conventional reaction procedure remains an important entry point, especially when a laboratory must work within a specific set of pumps, reactors, and control modules.

Large language models provide an interface between such procedural descriptions and computational tools. ChemCrow and Coscientist combine chemical reasoning with tool use and experimentation, and El Agente coordinates computational chemistry workflows.[[38-40]] Co-Scientist and Paper2Agent extend collaborative scientific reasoning and the reuse of computational methods.[[41,42]] For laboratory procedures, ACRA links literature instructions to executable chemical descriptions and available hardware; AutoLabs combines clarification, calculation, and self-correction for liquid handling; and PRISM incorporates simulation feedback before robotic execution.[[43-45]] Together, these studies show how language-based interaction can connect scientific intent to structured operations.

Several systems address flow chemistry directly. Chat-microreactor uses literature-derived information for flow-pattern prediction and microreactor design guidance, including a piperacillin application.[[46]] SapoMind combines condition recommendation with experimental optimization of continuous-flow lanolin saponification.[[47]] Ruan et al. developed LLM-RDF for end-to-end synthesis development, integrating experimental execution, kinetic analysis, optimization, purification, and reactor-design tasks.[[48]] These studies establish both quantitative engineering and experimental development as part of the emerging scope of chemical agents. For batch-to-flow translation, the practical task is to bring these decisions together in a connected process whose feed compositions, reactor conditions, and equipment assignments can be inspected as a whole.

Related work in chemical process design provides complementary approaches to this integration. Tian et al. generate process topologies and parameters for executable simulation flowsheets,[[49]] and the CeProAgents preprint organizes knowledge extraction, conceptual design, and parameter optimization into specialist agent groups.[[50]] The CAAF preprint investigates deterministic constraint checking and candidate revision, including a constructed flow-reactor design problem.[[51]] The El Agente Gráfico and La Agente Óptima preprints from the Aspuru-Guzik group further explore structured execution and agent-supervised optimization.[[52,53]] FlowPilot builds on this broader direction while focusing on the relationship between a reaction procedure and its laboratory-specific flow configuration: which streams enter each stage, how their compositions and flow rates determine operating conditions, and which available instruments can realize the connected design. Assessing this capability requires more than fluent explanations or plausible individual settings. Chemical reasoning benchmarks have identified elementary errors and overconfidence in otherwise capable models,[[54]] and uncertainty-aware optimization studies have documented invalid suggestions within defined reaction search spaces.[[55]] For a flow process, the corresponding concern is whether the chemical sequence, material balance, reactor geometry, and operating procedure agree. Evaluation therefore needs to examine the consistency of the complete proposed process as well as its eventual chemical performance.

Here we present FlowPilot, a batch-to-flow design workflow that combines standardized chemist intake, chemistry-aware literature retrieval, deterministic engineering calculations, specialist council review, and inventory assignment. A shared final design record links the stream compositions and flow rates to stage conditions, equipment selections, and the process diagram. We evaluate retrieval alignment, compare delivered designs with matched one-shot generation using fixed criteria and separate LLM judges, and examine the effects of council components and candidate exploration.[[56]] Two laboratory implementations then test complementary aspects of connected-process design: photoredox Giese addition followed by oxidation requires staged oxygen introduction (Figure 5), whereas DPDTC-mediated amidation requires interstage reagent addition and transfer of a reactive intermediate without isolation (Figure 6).[[57,58]]

METHODS

Standardized inputs and study organization
Each design began with a batch protocol and a structured intake package recording the objective, chemistry identity, experimental history, laboratory inventory, operating limits, and chemist hypotheses. Follow-up questions used stable identifiers and predefined readiness rules; optional evidence could be explicitly marked unavailable. The confirmed package was frozen for the run, retaining the answers and their provenance. Measured observations, protocol facts, and hypotheses were stored separately, while declared equipment and operating limits constrained design feasibility. Computational comparisons used archived case packages rather than newly generated conversations, keeping the supplied chemistry and constraints constant between architectures. The intake contract and question rules are given in ESI Section 1 and Tables S1–S3. Benchmark campaigns and subsequent laboratory-design runs were treated as distinct software/configuration records; the dated development history is summarized in Table S35.

Literature corpus and plan-aware retrieval
The literature resource comprised 464 classified flow-chemistry records annotated with available reaction, reactor, material, operating-condition, yield, and inlet-stream information. Missing metadata were retained as missing rather than filled from model inference. A complementary handbook-derived store contained 2,537 engineering rules with topic, severity, source, and applicability information.[[3,4,21]] Relevant rules supplied context to chemistry interpretation; only checks implemented in the calculation and validation routines acted as deterministic constraints. Field completeness and rule coverage are reported in Tables S4–S5.

A chemistry plan was extracted from the intake package and used to construct a retrieval query from reaction class, mechanism, catalyst, solvent, phase regime, and operating conditions. The search began with paired batch-flow records under mechanism and phase filters. Filters were relaxed when fewer than three candidates remained, and the search expanded to all records if no paired match remained. Reranking combined 0.60 semantic similarity with 0.40 field similarity; the latter compared photocatalyst, solvent, wavelength, temperature, and concentration using the weights in Table S6. The designated source record for each benchmark was excluded before final selection. This controlled direct retrieval of the reference, not possible exposure during model pretraining. Retrieval was evaluated by rank changes and top-five photocatalyst-family matching against semantic search over the frozen 1,600 query-result pairs (Table S7; ESI Section 2).

Engineering calculations and stage accounting
The engineering layer recalculated quantities dependent on proposed operating choices. Component molar delivery was obtained from concentration and liquid volumetric flow, and each stage received the cumulative flow of the streams introduced at or upstream of that stage. For a nominal liquid-filled reactor, the stage time was calculated as reactor volume divided by local liquid flow. An interstage feed therefore changed the downstream material balance and time calculation without changing the upstream flow. Packed-bed empty volume, estimated liquid holdup, and catalyst-contact time were distinguished where applicable. Stage totals were formed only from times reported on compatible bases.

Gas equivalents were calculated from the reactive-gas molar flow divided by the limiting-reactant molar flow, including gas composition and the stated reference temperature and pressure. The proposal STP convention was 273.15 K and 1 atm. Where required by a benchmark contract, in-channel gas volume was derived by ideal-gas scaling using absolute operating pressure and temperature. The inlet-reference index V/(Qliquid + Qgas,STP) was kept separate from pressure-corrected apparent time and from an experimentally measured residence time. Pressure-loss and thermal screening used the selected geometry, phase assumptions, and available material properties; estimated heat-transfer or reaction-enthalpy inputs were identified as assumptions. The implemented quantities, multiphase conventions, and heat-transfer screening are detailed in ESI Section 4 and Table S13.

Candidate generation and council review
The downstream Designer generated alternative geometries and operating choices from the chemistry plan, retrieved analogies, engineering calculations, and inventory constraints. The Chemistry, Kinetics, Fluidics, and Safety specialists reviewed chemical compatibility, reaction-time plausibility, transport and hardware feasibility, and hazards, respectively. The Skeptic examined assumptions and contradictions, and the Orchestrator selected among the reviewed candidates. Agent verdicts and proposed revisions were stored with candidate identifiers. Changes to independent variables triggered deterministic recalculation before a candidate could be compared or published. Candidate and revision budgets followed the configuration recorded for each campaign rather than being inferred from the final output. Rejected alternatives and revision history were retained alongside the selected candidate. Council terminology, configuration studies, and examples of actual deliberation are documented in ESI Sections 6.1 and 7.5.

Inventory realization and result publication
Inventory profiles were imported as structured data or normalized from laboratory documents into typed equipment records with stable identifiers. Available quantities, system membership, material, reactor volume and diameter, pump range, pressure and temperature limits, and compatible light modules were recorded where supplied. Realization assigned required operations to available items and checked the declared capability and connectivity constraints. Calculations were repeated when equipment selection changed a dependent quantity. An unresolved essential requirement was retained as a diagnostic requirement, not silently replaced with invented hardware (Table S14).

The final design contract reconciled the selected proposal, stage table, stream table, equipment allocation, and topology. GUI numerical views and the executable process diagram were derived from that record; earlier calculations and council exchanges remained separately available for inspection. Timestamped run folders stored the input package, result, final contract, topology, equipment allocation, and available diagram artifacts. Experimental feedback was linked to the conditions actually tested before being supplied to a later design cycle. ESI Sections 1.4–1.5 and 7 document the GUI and saved artifacts, and Tables S15–S16 specify the feedback and provenance records. Exact computational replay additionally requires the model, decoding settings, dependencies, and software revision recorded for the campaign.

Matched architecture comparison and independent judging
The primary comparison used CuAAC, photochemical oxidation, and hydrogenolysis case packages with the same protocol, objective, inventory, and final-output contract for both architectures. One-shot generation received this information in a single call; FlowPilot distributed it across its interpretation, calculation, review, and realization stages. Qwen3.6-27B, Qwen3.8-27B, GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and Claude Opus 4.6 were each used with both architectures for three generation repeats per case, giving 90 outcomes at the recorded generation temperature of 0.2. The archived GPT-5.4 generator results additionally displayed in Figure 4b–c were not included in these five-model aggregates or Tables S11–S12. Exact case prompts, inventories, and model records are retained in ESI Section 3. The comparison concerns delivered design quality under matched task information, not equal token or computation budgets.

For judging, final designs were normalized into candidate packets with generator and architecture labels removed. Separate Qwen, OpenAI, and Claude judge calls received the candidate, common input context, reference facts, deterministic verification evidence, and the fixed 14-criterion rubric. The archived judge configuration used Qwen3.6-27B, GPT-5.4, and Claude Sonnet 4.6 at temperature 0.0; these evaluator calls were separate from candidate generation. Each applicable criterion received an integer rating from 0 to 4. Ratings were averaged across judges for each criterion, then across applicable criteria, and divided by four to give a score from 0 to 1. All judges and applicable criteria had equal weight. Gas bookkeeping and multistage closure could be excluded only when their applicability conditions were false; missing required information was scored rather than treated as inapplicable. Critical flags were counted separately from the numerical score. Deterministic evidence did not confer an architecture-specific bonus, and identity masking did not guarantee that output style concealed the architecture. Scoring anchors, criterion definitions, and applicability rules are specified in Tables S8–S9.

Ablation experiments and statistical reporting
For each generator and architecture, the three case scores were averaged within each repeat. Reported architecture error bars are the sample standard deviation of these three repeat-level means, not the dispersion of all nine individual outcomes. Critical-flag counts were summed across the three cases within a repeat before calculating their mean and sample standard deviation. The aggregate values in Tables S11–S12 use only the five-model cohort. Generation cost was estimated from recorded token usage and the archived model-specific price schedule, excluding evaluator calls and deployment costs. Figure 4e uses mean score divided by mean generation cost; the case-specific ESI plots instead summarize per-outcome score-to-cost ratios (Figure S10).

The internal ablation screen fixed the generator to Qwen3.8-27B and tested 15 conditions over the same three cases, producing 45 outcomes with one generation per condition-case pair. The conditions removed or modified council components, revision stages, selection, or candidate budgets. Disabled specialists received no substitute score, and active council weights were renormalized. The resulting standard deviations describe between-case variation, not repeatability; all conditions are reported in Table S10. A separate historical study crossed four upstream and four council endpoints and tested candidate budgets of 1, 6, 12, and 24 with five repeats. Its engineering-profile metrics were normalized independently of the LLM-judge rubric (ESI Section 6.1; Tables S18–S21). No experimental yield uncertainty was inferred from these computational repeats.

Laboratory implementation and chemical analysis
KHU implemented the two connected-stage cases using a Vapourtec E-series system with sample-loop injection. For the Giese addition/oxidation sequence, a PFA coil in a 450 nm UV-150 module was followed by a separately illuminated FEP coil. The substrate/acrylonitrile/iridium-catalyst feed was prepared for the oxygen-free first stage; pure oxygen was introduced through an MFC at the interstage mixer, and both reactors were maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. For DPDTC-mediated amidation, the acid/DPDTC/DMAP solution and benzylamine solution were delivered by separate channels, with amine addition between two ETFE coils maintained at 95 °C. Both setups used a 7 bar cartridge BPR; recorded system pressure was reported separately from the cartridge rating. Actual concentrations, molar feeds, operating points, and proposal-to-implementation differences are tabulated in ESI Section 12 and Tables S31, S34, and S38–S39.

NMR yields were reported using 1,3-benzodioxole as the internal standard. Isolated products were characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy and low-resolution mass spectrometry. Shared response-set entries in the supplied laboratory record were treated as grouped operating points, not independent replicates, and no experimental standard deviation was assigned without individual measurements. ESI Section 12 contains the feed preparation, work-up, irradiation specifications, apparatus photographs, and characterization records; Figures S28–S31 reproduce the product spectra. MFC reference-state confirmation, collection windows, and quantitative-NMR calculation records are identified there as outstanding metadata rather than filled by inference.
