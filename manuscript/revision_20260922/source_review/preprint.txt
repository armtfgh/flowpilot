FlowPilot: A Multi-Agent AI System for Continuous
Flow Chemistry Process Design
Amirreza Mottafegh, Gwang-Noh Ahn
Abstract
Designing a continuous ﬂow process demands coordinated reasoning across ﬂuid dynamics, heat
and mass transfer, materials compatibility, and safety, a multi-domain problem exceeding the
reliable scope of a single-shot large language model (LLM). We present FlowPilot, to our knowledge
the ﬁrst multi-agent AI pipeline for sustainable continuous ﬂow process design. LLM agents
interpret chemistry and mechanism, while a nine-step deterministic calculator and approximately
2,500 handbook-derived design rules perform all quantitative computation. A seven-agent Council
deliberates over candidate designs under bounded domain authority, selecting a ﬁnal design via
a weighted Process Value Score quantifying improvement over batch. Benchmarking across a 4×4
matrix of commercial and local LLM endpoints shows that the council contributes measurable
design quality beyond model capability alone. We validate FlowPilot on an isoxazole cycloaddition
benchmark and two end-to-end case studies: a photoredox Giese addition with aerobic oxidation
and a thermal α-bromination with inline quench.

ABSTRACT

mass transfer, materials compatibility, and safety, a multi-domain problem exceeding the reliable scope of
a single-shot large language model (LLM). We present FlowPilot, to our knowledge the first multi-agent
AI  pipeline  for  sustainable  continuous  flow  process  design.  LLM  agents  interpret  chemistry  and
mechanism, while a nine-step deterministic calculator and approximately 2,500 handbook-derived design
rules perform all quantitative computation. A seven-agent Council deliberates over candidate designs
under bounded domain authority, selecting a final design via a weighted Process Value Score quantifying
improvement over batch. Benchmarking across a 4×4 matrix of commercial and local LLM endpoints

FlowPilot on an isoxazole cycloaddition benchmark and two end-to-end case studies: a photoredox Giese
addition with aerobic oxidation and a thermal α-bromination with inline quench.
INTRODUCTION
Continuous flow chemistry has matured into one of the main technologies1,2 for pharmaceutical3, fine-
chemical4, and on-demand manufacturing5, offering orders-of-magnitude improvements in heat and mass
transfer6, intrinsically safer handling of hazardous intermediates7, access to short residence times2, and
ready accommodation of photochemistry8, electrochemistry9, liquid-phase oxidation10, and multi-step
coupling11–13. These intensified conditions translate directly into reduced solvent consumption, lower
energy input, and smaller equipment footprints, aligning flow synthesis with the resource-efficiency goals
of sustainable manufacturing. A mature commercial ecosystem now supplies virtually everything a flow
chemist needs14, yet adoption remains gated less by what flow can do than by what it costs to design: the
gap between a published batch protocol and a working flow process is typically closed by a small team
over weeks of iteration; even within established translation paradigms such as microwave-to-flow, each

PAGE 3
new chemistry class typically requires re-derivation of operating conditions15. The bottleneck has shifted
from running flow chemistry to designing flow processes.
Designing a flow process is a chain of interlocking decisions across fluid dynamics16,17, mass transfer18,
heat transfer19, materials compatibility, kinetics, and safety, where each decision constrains the next20,21.
This coupled, multi-domain design surface is forbidding for an individual chemist. Existing tools address
fragments of it: classical process simulators22 solve the engineering rigorously but require a pre-specified
flowsheet, and reinforcement-learning approaches relax this restriction only under simplified process
abstractions23; Bayesian and active-learning optimizers24–28 refine a known process but cannot propose
one;  retrosynthesis  tools29–31 answer  what  to  make  but  not  how  to  run  it  in  flow.  Handbook
knowledge20,21,32 encodes the rules of thumb an experienced flow chemist relies on, but this knowledge is
dispersed  across  textbook  chapters,  qualitative  in  form,  and  almost  never  operationalized
computationally. No current tool designs a flow process from a chemistry brief while honoring these
handbook-level fundamentals.
Recent large-language-model (LLM) copilots for chemistry33,34 have expanded what AI can do for a
chemist, from synthesis planning to autonomous experimentation35–38, but these efforts remain largely
batch-oriented. When a monolithic LLM is prompted directly for a flow reactor design, it must handle
chemistry, kinetics, fluid mechanics, heat transfer, and safety within a single inference pass 39,40, with no
mechanism to enforce cross-domain consistency. The predictable outcome is internally inconsistent
outputs and no traceable audit of which constraints were evaluated.
The structure of flow process design, a coupled problem solved by a team of complementary specialists,
maps naturally onto a multi-agent architecture in which each agent owns one domain and a separate
orchestrator integrates their judgments. The organizing principle of this work is therefore: LLMs interpret
chemistry; deterministic code, anchored in handbook fundamentals, computes.
We introduce FlowPilot, to our knowledge the first multi-agent AI pipeline purpose-built for continuous
flow chemistry process design. Given a batch protocol or a target reaction, FlowPilot returns a defensible,
intensification-aware flow process design accompanied by a process flow diagram, traceable engineering
calculations,  mechanism-matched  literature  analogies,  and  explicit  uncertainty  flags.  The  system
comprises five coupled stages: (i) a chemistry-interpretation agent that produces a structured plan
including an IntensificationMandate, a computable target that quantifies the minimum residence-time
reduction, throughput gain, and intensification factor a flow design must deliver over the published batch
protocol; (ii) plan-aware retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) against a curated flow-chemistry corpus,

PAGE 4
in which a chemistry-conditioned query expansion replaces single-pass semantic search; (iii) a nine-step
deterministic engineering calculator grounded in approximately 2,500 handbook-derived design rules; (iv)
a seven-agent Council that audits candidate designs under bounded domain authority and selects a final
design through a weighted, internally computed score including an explicit Process Value Score (used
only by the orchestrator); and (v) automatic rendering of a chemistry-aware process flow diagram.
This work makes the following contributions. We present the FlowPilot architecture and its hybrid LLM-
deterministic design. We introduce a computable IntensificationMandate that converts the qualitative
question of whether flow improves on batch into a quantitative design gate. We benchmark FlowPilot on
a 4×4 matrix of upstream and council models spanning commercial endpoints (Claude Sonnet, GPT-4o,
GPT-4o-mini) and a local model (Gemma 4 31B), yielding 16 completed pairings including lightweight
modes, and demonstrate through pre/post-council comparison that the council contributes measurable
design quality beyond model capability alone. Finally, we validate the system on an isoxazole 1,3-dipolar
cycloaddition  benchmark41 and  two  end-to-end  case  studies,  a  photoredox  Giese  addition  with
downstream aerobic oxidation42 and a thermal α-bromination with inline quench43.
RESULTS
FlowPilot was designed to translate a batch chemistry protocol into a continuous-flow process proposal
while  keeping  chemical  interpretation,  deterministic  engineering  calculation,  and  expert  review  as
separate operations (Figure 1). The input layer accepts either a user query, a batch protocol, a chemistry
goal, or a laboratory inventory. The upstream module then converts this unstructured input into a
chemistry  plan,  including  species  identification,  reaction  classification,  stream-separation  logic,
sensitivity flags, and an explicit statement of why flow should offer value over batch. This chemistry plan
is passed to a deterministic design calculator that generates the initial engineering proposal, including
residence time, reactor geometry, stream assignments, and operating conditions.

The downstream council provides the main error-control mechanism. Rather than asking one model to
produce a single final answer, the system generates a candidate pool and distributes review across
domain-specialized agents. The Designer proposes candidate geometries and operating points; Chemistry
Agent evaluates chemical compatibility, photochemical constraints, and stream logic; Kinetics Agent
evaluates residence time and conversion plausibility; Fluidics Agent evaluates flow regime, pressure
drop, mixing, and hardware compatibility; Safety Agent evaluates pressure, thermal, material, and
handling risks; the Skeptic audits assumptions and internal consistency; and the Orchestrator selects the

PAGE 6
final  candidate.  This  structure  produces  not  only  a  selected  design  but  also  a  record  of  rejected
alternatives, revised candidates, disqualified candidates, and the rationale for each decision.
The output of the workflow is a validated design package rather than only a text answer. The final
response includes a process-flow diagram, an evaluation overview, protocol and report summaries,
confidence and safety flags, and citations or retrieval-backed analogies where available. The system
therefore exposes both the final operating point and the path by which that point was selected.
Literature and rule-base coverage
The literature dataset used for retrieval and knowledge extraction covers a broad range of flow-chemistry
applications (Figure 2). The curated corpus contained annotated query–record pairs drawn from 464
papers spanning thermal synthesis, photoredox catalysis, heterogeneous photocatalysis, cross-coupling,
oxidation/reduction,  and  other  reaction  classes.  Reactor  types  ranged  from  capillary/coil  and
microreactor/chip systems to photoreactors and packed-bed reactors, with fluoropolymer tubing (PTFE,
PFA, FEP) dominant among materials. Most annotated processes used two or three inlet streams,
supporting the system’s default topology assumptions. This diversity matters because the benchmark and
case-study reactions include both photochemical and non-photochemical processes, and the retrieval stage
must therefore recognize different reactor classes, materials, and operating regimes.

The engineering knowledge base contained 2,537 structured rules spanning reactor design, heat transfer,
catalyst behavior, residence time, photochemistry, materials, scale-up, pressure, mixing, and safety
(Figure 2e-f). Coverage was not uniform across chemistry classes: heat-transfer rules were densely
associated with thermal synthesis and cross-coupling, whereas photochemistry rules concentrated in
photoredox and photocycloaddition classes. This structured rule landscape constrains model-generated
designs with flow-chemistry fundamentals instead of relying only on model priors.

Plan-aware retrieval improves analogy quality
The retrieval system was evaluated against a standard semantic-search baseline (Figure 3). A standard
RAG pipeline embeds the raw batch protocol or query summary and ranks records by cosine similarity.
FlowPilot instead expands the query using the chemistry plan, including reaction class, photocatalyst
family, solvent, wavelength, and mechanistic constraints. Candidate records are retrieved through a three-
tier filtering strategy (Figure 3a): the first tier applies hard metadata filters (e.g., reaction class), the
second relaxes these filters to broaden coverage, and the third falls back to pure semantic retrieval when
no metadata match is found. Surviving candidates are then reranked by a weighted field score that
rewards mechanistically relevant matches.

filter, and pure semantic fallback. (b) Query enrichment expands chemically specific search terms

The enriched query representation approximately doubled the number of chemically specific search terms
in the two examples shown in Figure 3b. A photoredox-catalysis query increased from 7 naive terms to 15
FlowPilot terms, and a photocatalytic radical difunctionalization query increased from 7 to 14 terms. In
the retrieval-pair benchmark, 1,600 query–result pairs were evaluated. FlowPilot reranked 25.1% of these
1,600 pairs relative to the pure semantic baseline, indicating that field-aware scoring materially changed
which analogies were presented to the design engine.
The  strongest  retrieval  improvement  was  observed  for  photocatalyst-family  matching.  For  iridium
systems, top-5 family matching increased from 40.0% under semantic search to 95.0% under FlowPilot
reranking, a 55 percentage-point improvement. Organic-dye systems improved from 42.7% to 90.7%,
ruthenium systems from 77.3% to 94.7%, TiO2 systems from 87.1% to 98.6%, and ZnO systems from
45.0% to 60.0%. The demonstration query in Figure 3d illustrates the mechanism: semantic search
retrieved  superficially  similar  photochemical  examples,  whereas  FlowPilot  preferentially  promoted
records with the same photocatalyst family and closer wavelength context. This matters for downstream
design because residence time, optical path length, material selection, and LED wavelength are not
interchangeable across photoredox families.
Council review reveals model-dependent design behavior
The  FlowPilot  architecture  was  benchmarked  on  an  isoxazole  1,3-dipolar  cycloaddition41 as  a
representative test reaction, using a model-matrix experiment in which the upstream model and council
model were varied independently (Figure 4). The final benchmark contained 16 completed model pairings
after including lightweight upstream modes for GPT-4o-mini and Gemma 4. Across this matrix, all runs
returned validated designs, but the selected operating points depended strongly on the upstream model.

The council model primarily affected execution cost and latency rather than final design quality. Claude
and GPT-4o councils completed runs roughly three times faster than GPT-4o-mini and Gemma 4
councils, which required more corrective iterations (Figure 4c).
The upstream model exerted a stronger effect on the final design. Claude and GPT-4o upstreams
consistently produced compact, short-residence-time designs in the single-digit-minute range, whereas
Gemma 4 upstreams generated designs an order of magnitude longer and more variable, with reactor
volumes several times larger (Figure 4d). The downstream council can therefore repair and validate weak-
model outputs, but the upstream chemistry interpretation still strongly constrains the design region
explored by the downstream agents.

The model/manuscript overlay further shows that different model combinations occupy distinct regions of
design space (Figure 4d). Strong-model combinations clustered near compact, small-ID reactors, while

low-productivity  references  define  a  broad  acceptable  region  rather  than  a  single  unique  target,
reinforcing that FlowPilot designs are candidate operating points to be validated experimentally rather
than exact reproductions of one literature condition.
Candidate budget controls exploration breadth
Candidate-budget experiments tested whether giving the council more candidate designs improves final
output (Figure 4e-f). Budgets of B = 1, 6, 12, and 24 were evaluated with five repeats each. Increasing the
budget broadened the explored design families; from a single cluster at B = 1 to a mixture of compact
high-flowrate, large-ID, and long-residence-time solutions at B = 24; but did not produce monotonically
better final designs. At the highest budget, the council occasionally selected outlier long-residence-time
solutions, driving up both the mean and variance of design metrics.
Revision activity and deliberation workload scaled roughly linearly with budget, while disqualification
rates also rose as the council audited a larger candidate pool. The practical conclusion is that candidate
budget should be treated as an exploration parameter: a moderate budget (B = 6–12) offers a robust first
design, whereas a high budget is useful when the user explicitly wants broader alternative discovery.
Case study 1: photoredox Giese addition followed by aerobic oxidation
The first end-to-end case study was a one-pot photoredox Giese addition followed by aerobic oxidation to
a sulfoxide product42 (Figure 5). The batch protocol required strict oxygen exclusion in the first step,
followed by oxygen introduction in the second step. This presents a topology challenge: the system must
preserve an anaerobic photoredox radical-addition stage while enabling an aerobic oxidation stage
downstream.

FlowPilot  correctly  identified  the  need  for  a  staged  process.  The  generated  process-flow  diagram
contained two liquid feeds, an initial mixer, a degassing step, a first photoreactor, an MFC-controlled air
or oxygen feed into a second mixer, a second photoreactor, a back-pressure regulator, and product
collection. The system preserved the literature photocatalyst, solvent system, temperature, wavelength,
and two-stage logic. The design used Ir(dF(CF3)ppy)2(dtbpy)PF6 at 0.5 mol%, EtOH/pH 9 buffer (5:1
v/v), 25 °C operation, and 452 nm irradiation, matching the reported 450 nm literature wavelength to
within 2 nm.
The council deliberation exposed the trade-off that made this case difficult. The Designer generated 12
candidates, filtered out six low-conversion candidates, and retained three survivors. Chemistry Agent

PAGE 13
judged all three chemically plausible, noting that Beer-Lambert absorbance remained low risk. Kinetics
Agent  was  more  restrictive  and  accepted  only  one  candidate;  other  candidates  were  blocked  for
insufficient estimated conversion or unsupported intensification. Fluidics Agent found all three candidates
acceptable from pressure and Reynolds-number perspectives, but noted coil-length differences. Safety
Agent found no major thermal concern and accepted 2 bar BPR operation for the liquid system, while
flagging ethanol handling and the need to maintain the inert first stage. The Skeptic identified a medium-
level  inconsistency  in  one  Beer-Lambert  estimate  but  no  critical  error.  The  Orchestrator  selected
Candidate 1 with a combined score of 0.820.
Compared with the literature process, FlowPilot reproduced the qualitative process structure but selected
a more conservative hydraulic point. The final design used a liquid flow rate of 0.0685 mL/min, total
residence time of 124 min, and total reactor volume of 8.5 mL (two stages of 4.25 mL each). Because the
current deterministic calculator computes residence times on a liquid-phase basis and does not account for
gas-phase holdup in the aerobic second stage, we restrict the quantitative comparison to Stage 1, which is
a single-phase liquid system in both the FlowPilot design and the literature process. For Stage 1,
FlowPilot selected a residence time of 62 min and a reactor volume of 4.25 mL at a flow rate of 0.0685
mL/min, compared with the literature values of 40 min and 5 mL at 0.125 mL/min42. The system therefore
overestimated  the  Stage  1  residence  time  by  approximately  1.6-fold  and  operated  at  a  flow  rate
approximately  1.8-fold  lower,  while  proposing  a  reactor  volume  approximately  15%  smaller.  The
literature process additionally reports a 1.4 bar BPR versus the 2 bar selected by FlowPilot. This case
illustrates both the strength and limitation of the current system: it can infer a chemically correct staged
photochemical topology, but kinetic uncertainty in the absence of experimentally measured rate constants
drives conservative residence-time selection. Extending the calculator to handle gas–liquid multiphase
residence times is an identified direction for future work.
Case study 2: thermal alpha-bromination with inline quench
The second case study was thermal alpha-bromination of 3-phenylpropanal with bromine in MeCN,
followed by sodium thiosulfate quench43 (Figure 6). The batch protocol used 3-phenylpropanal and Br2 at
25 °C under N2, followed by aqueous Na2S2O3 quench and workup. The key design challenge was fast
mixing and controlled bromine addition rather than light delivery to a photocatalyst.

FlowPilot generated a two-stage flow topology consisting of substrate and bromine pumps, a T-mixer,
degassing, a first reactor, a quench pump, a second mixer, a quench/contact reactor, and product
collection. The selected design used MeCN at 25 °C, no BPR, and a two-stage process matching the
reported literature structure. The first reactor was assigned tau = 2.2 min and VR = 5.30 mL, and the
quench/contact reactor was assigned tau = 1.0 min and VR = 2.51 mL. The total flow rate was 2.5
mL/min, and the total residence time was 3.2 min.
The council log shows that the agents correctly recognized this as a mixing-dominated problem. The
Designer noted that all 12 candidates carried a dual-criterion mixing warning, but none were immediately
disqualified. Chemistry Agent preferred shorter tau values with 0.75 mm ID tubing to minimize side
reactions and emphasized continuous N2 atmosphere. Kinetics Agent found that all candidates achieved

PAGE 15
adequate estimated conversion, with X = 0.98-1.00 and an intensification factor of approximately 10
relative to the 30 min batch time. Fluidics Agent flagged the dominant unresolved issue: every candidate
failed the mixing criterion (rmix > 0.20), suggesting that smaller tubing ID or improved micromixing
should be considered. Safety Agent found no thermal or pressure risk but required standard MeCN
handling controls and continuous N2. The Orchestrator selected Candidate 11 with a combined score of
0.756, primarily because it had the best geometry score among otherwise closely ranked alternatives.
The literature comparison shows strong agreement in qualitative process design and residence-time scale.
FlowPilot matched the solvent, temperature, two-stage design, and absence of BPR. Its total residence
time of 3.2 min fell within the reported 2-4 min literature range. The main discrepancy was scale: the
literature process used approximately 30 mL/min and approximately 80 mL reactor volume, whereas
FlowPilot proposed 2.5 mL/min and 7.8 mL. This is a scale difference rather than a chemistry-design
mismatch. The case also demonstrates why council logs are useful: even when the final design agrees
with literature residence time, the system preserves the unresolved mixing concern as an explicit warning
rather than hiding it behind the final score.
DISCUSSION
The hybrid architecture separates chemical judgment from engineering arithmetic
The results above demonstrate that decomposing flow process design into two distinct reasoning modes;
chemical  interpretation  by  LLM  agents  and  quantitative computation by a deterministic calculator
grounded in handbook rules; addresses a structural weakness of monolithic LLM approaches. When a
single model must simultaneously decide on a reaction mechanism, estimate a residence time, compute a
Reynolds number, and size a back-pressure regulator, the outputs are often internally inconsistent because
no enforcement mechanism checks that each quantity follows from a shared set of physical assumptions.
The FlowPilot architecture removes this failure mode by construction: the nine-step calculator derives
every engineering quantity from  a  common  set of  inputs  (flow rate, diameter,  solvent  properties,
temperature),  and  LLM  agents  are  not  permitted  to  override  these  values.  The  pre/post-council
comparison in Figure 4b illustrates the practical consequence. The upstream candidate carried weak plug-
flow quality and a low intensification factor; after council review, the design shifted to a smaller tubing
diameter and shorter residence time, nearly tripling the normalized radar-area score (Figure 4b). The
council did not merely rephrase the upstream proposal; it materially revised the operating point by
applying domain-specific constraints that the upstream model had not enforced. The α-bromination case
study further shows that the council preserves unresolved engineering concerns, such as the mixing-

PAGE 16
criterion violation flagged by Fluidics Agent, as explicit warnings in the final output instead of absorbing
them into a confident summary. This transparency is what distinguishes a co-pilot from a black-box
recommendation engine.
Positioning relative to prior AI systems for chemistry
Several recent systems have applied LLMs to chemistry tasks: ChemCrow34 augments an LLM with tool
calls  for  property  lookup  and  safety  assessment  but  operates  in  the  batch  domain;  Coscientist 38
demonstrated autonomous experiment execution but targets the laboratory loop rather than process
design; El Agente35 automates quantum-chemistry calculations at the electronic-structure layer; Zeng et
al.34 applied a multi-agent framework to process optimization but assumes an already-defined process;
and Materealize37 employs multi-agent deliberation for materials design but does not incorporate fluid-
dynamics or heat-transfer calculations. Classical process simulators22 require a pre-specified flowsheet
and do not reason about chemistry, while Bayesian optimizers24–28 refine known processes but cannot
propose one. FlowPilot addresses the segment between a chemistry brief and a defensible first flow
design: it reasons about mechanism, generates engineering calculations, and audits the result through
domain-specialist  agents,  producing  a  starting  point  for  downstream  experimental  optimization  or
rigorous simulation.
The council architecture contributes beyond raw model capability
The model-matrix benchmark provides direct evidence that the council adds value beyond single-model
inference. Across the 4×4 matrix, the upstream model determined the design region (residence time,
reactor  volume,  tubing  diameter),  while  the  council  model  primarily  affected  execution  cost  and
corrective iterations. Even Gemma upstream runs, which produced highly variable initial designs, yielded
validated outputs after council review, indicating that the council acts as a quality floor rather than a pass-
through.
The candidate-budget experiments add a second dimension. Increasing the pool from B = 1 to B = 24
broadened the explored design space but did not produce monotonically better final designs; at high
budgets,  the  council  occasionally  selected  long-residence-time  solutions.  This  is  consistent  with
constrained search rather than unconstrained optimization. In practice, a moderate budget (B = 6–12)
offers a reasonable trade-off between exploration breadth and design robustness.

Limitations and scope boundaries
Several limitations should be noted. First, FlowPilot proposes designs but does not execute experiments.
Closed-loop self-driving laboratories that integrate flow synthesis with AI-guided experimentation44 could
in principle complete this validation step, and combining FlowPilot with such platforms is a natural next
step; nonetheless, experimental validation by a chemist remains essential at the design-evaluation stage.
The 1.6-fold Stage 1 residence-time overestimate in Case Study 1 illustrates the consequence of kinetic
uncertainty: without experimentally measured rate constants, the system relies on LLM-estimated kinetic
anchors that tend toward conservative values.
Second, the deterministic calculator currently computes residence times on a liquid-phase basis and does
not model gas-phase holdup in gas–liquid multiphase stages. For processes involving gaseous co-feeds
such as the aerobic oxidation in Case Study 1, the reported residence times and reactor volumes reflect
the liquid flow contribution only. Incorporating gas–liquid slug-flow models45 would improve the fidelity
of multiphase designs.
Third, the engineering rule base (2,537 rules) is not uniformly dense across chemistry classes. Thermal
synthesis and photoredox catalysis are well represented, but electrochemistry, biocatalysis, and gas-phase
flow chemistry have sparser coverage, which may reduce audit-trail reliability in those domains.
Fourth, the current benchmarks involve single-reaction or two-stage processes. Complex multi-step
telescopes, where yield losses accumulate across stages, have not been evaluated and would require
extensions to the topology builder and inter-stage consistency checks.
Fifth, frontier commercial models (Claude, GPT-4o) produced more consistent designs than the local
Gemma 4 model, which required rescue pathways. Defining an acceptable quality floor for fully self-
hosted deployment remains an open question.
Outlook
The  most  immediate  extension  is  a  closed-loop  workflow  in  which  FlowPilot  designs  are  tested
experimentally and the results are fed back to update kinetic anchors, reducing the conservatism observed
in Case Study 1 and connecting the co-pilot to existing Bayesian optimization tools for flow-condition
refinement24–28. Expanding the rule base to electrochemistry, biocatalysis, and gas-liquid classes would
broaden coverage. Because the deterministic calculator already tracks reactor volume, solvent inventory,
and energy-related parameters (residence time, temperature, pressure drop), sustainability metrics such as
E-factor and process mass intensity can be computed directly from the design output, connecting AI-

PAGE 18
assisted process design to quantitative green chemistry evaluation. Supporting longer multi-step reaction
telescopes and automated scale-up estimation from laboratory to pilot scale are further directions that the
modular architecture is designed to accommodate.
CONCLUSION
We have presented FlowPilot, to our knowledge the first multi-agent AI system that translates a chemistry
brief into a defensible continuous flow process design. The architecture enforces a strict division of labor:
LLM agents interpret chemistry and mechanism, a nine-step deterministic calculator owns all engineering
quantities, a handbook-derived rule base of approximately 2,500 rules supplies the domain constraints
that experienced flow chemists rely on, and a seven-agent Council audits candidate designs under
bounded domain authority. This separation is not incidental but structural; it prevents the internal
inconsistencies that arise when a single model must simultaneously generate Reynolds numbers, pressure
drops, residence times, and safety assessments without a shared physical basis.
Benchmarking  across  a  4×4  model  matrix  and  candidate-budget  sweeps  shows  that  the  council
contributes measurable design quality beyond model capability alone, acting as a quality floor that revises
weak upstream proposals instead of passing them through. The two end-to-end case studies, a photoredox
Giese addition with downstream aerobic oxidation and a thermal α-bromination with inline quench,
demonstrate that FlowPilot can recover chemically meaningful process topologies and residence-time
scales consistent with published flow processes, while preserving unresolved engineering concerns as
explicit warnings rather than absorbing them into a confident summary.
The practical implication for the flow chemistry community is a shift in the design bottleneck. Translating
a batch protocol into a first flow design currently requires a team of specialists iterating over weeks;
FlowPilot compresses this to a co-pilot proposal generated in minutes, with every number traceable to an
equation,  rule,  or  chemistry  judgment.  By  systematically  favoring  intensified  conditions;  shorter
residence times, smaller reactor volumes, and reduced solvent inventories; the system aligns AI-assisted
process  design  with  the  resource-efficiency  goals  that  motivate  the  adoption  of  continuous  flow
manufacturing. The system does not replace experimental validation, which remains essential, but it
provides an auditable starting point from which experimental refinement can begin.
More  broadly,  the  hybrid  pattern  demonstrated  here,  LLM-driven  interpretation  coupled  with
deterministic computation and multi-agent cross-domain audit, is not specific to flow chemistry. Any
engineering design problem that is currently solved by a small team of complementary specialists

iterating across coupled domains is a candidate for the same architectural approach. The modular design
of FlowPilot is intended to make such extensions practical.
METHODS
A literature corpus of 464 flow-chemistry papers was assembled and annotated with structured metadata
(reaction class, reactor type, material, residence time, flow rate, yield, and stream count). From this
corpus and from established flow-chemistry handbooks20,21,32, an engineering knowledge base of 2,537
rules was extracted and categorized by topic and severity.
The plan-aware retrieval pipeline first generates a chemistry plan from the user input and then constructs
an expanded query containing reaction class, mechanism, photocatalyst family, and sensitivity flags.
Retrieved candidates are reranked by a field-aware score that rewards mechanistic relevance over
superficial semantic similarity.
The  batch-to-flow  translation  workflow  comprises  chemistry  interpretation,  literature  retrieval,
deterministic engineering calculation, candidate generation, council review, and topology rendering. A
computable IntensificationMandate prevents acceptance of designs that do not measurably improve on
batch. The nine-step deterministic calculator owns all numerical quantities (Re, Pe, Da, ΔP, VP, VR, τ, IF, UA,
pressure headroom); LLM agents are not permitted to override these values.
The council reviews a candidate pool under bounded domain authority. Each of the seven agents
(Designer, Chemistry Agent, Kinetics Agent, Fluidics Agent, Safety Agent, Skeptic, Orchestrator) may
only modify fields within its own domain. Revised candidates are recomputed deterministically before
final scoring.
The model-matrix benchmark evaluated 16 upstream/council pairings across Claude, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-
mini, and Gemma 4. The candidate-budget benchmark tested B = 1, 6, 12, and 24 with five repeats each.
Two end-to-end case studies42,43 were compared against published flow processes on topology, operating
conditions, and residence-time scale.
