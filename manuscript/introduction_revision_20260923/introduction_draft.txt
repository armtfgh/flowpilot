A batch protocol describes what was done in a vessel; a continuous-flow design must also specify how materials move through a connected process. This distinction matters because the advantages of flow chemistry arise from the interaction between chemistry and reactor operation, not from miniaturization alone.[[2,3,22,23]] Controlled heat and mass transfer, irradiation, electrochemical interfaces, and gas delivery have enabled pharmaceutical synthesis and transformations that are difficult to operate reproducibly in batch.[[5-10]] Multistep and on-demand systems further demonstrate the value of coordinating reaction stages with downstream operations.[[4,11-13]] Nevertheless, available hardware and a successful batch procedure do not uniquely determine a suitable flow configuration.[[14,15]] The practical challenge is to turn chemical intent into a process specification whose reaction sequence, feeds, operating conditions, and equipment remain mutually consistent.

The decisions are coupled across length scales and stages. Reactor geometry affects transport and pressure loss; heating and mixing affect the conditions experienced by the reacting stream; and an interstage addition changes both composition and the volumetric flow entering the next reactor.[[18-21,34]] Consequently, a residence time cannot be selected independently of reactor volume, stream flow rates, and the basis used to describe a multiphase mixture. Gas equivalents require a molar-flow calculation rather than a comparison of gas and liquid volumetric rates.[[49]] These dependencies become especially important in telescoped chemistry, where incomplete upstream conversion changes the material available to the downstream reaction. Integrated apixaban synthesis and photochemical routes to sartan intermediates illustrate the chemical and operational coordination required in practical multistep development.[[16,17]]

Digital tools already support substantial parts of this task. Computer-aided synthesis planning proposes routes,[[31-33]] process simulation evaluates specified configurations,[[24,25]] and Bayesian or active-learning strategies guide experimental optimization.[[26-30]] AI-informed robotic flow synthesis has also connected route planning to physical execution.[[48]] More recently, RoboChem-Flex demonstrated modular closed-loop and human-in-the-loop reaction optimization across multiple chemistry classes, while UV-visible auto-reactometry coupled to LLM-assisted optimization expanded parallel photochemical screening.[[51,41]] These developments establish that automation and optimization in flow are already well advanced. They also motivate a complementary question: how can a chemist obtain an inspectable initial process proposal from a batch procedure and a laboratory-specific equipment inventory, before committing to an experimental campaign?

Large language models (LLMs) offer an interface between unstructured scientific descriptions and the tools needed to address that question. ChemCrow augmented a language model with chemistry tools, and Coscientist combined planning, information access, and experimental capabilities for autonomous chemical research.[[36,40]] Work from the Aspuru-Guzik group has extended this approach into specialist computational workflows: El Agente orchestrates quantum-chemistry calculations, and El Agente Cuantico coordinates software-specific agents for quantum simulations.[[37,52]] The important transition is from generating an answer to organizing actions that can be executed and inspected. However, the demonstrated competence of an agent in one scientific task does not establish competence in another; simulation workflows, synthesis planning, and the design of a pressurized multistage flow process require different tools and validation evidence.

Recent scientific-agent studies make both the opportunity and the evaluation challenge clearer. The Virtual Lab used specialist agents and human guidance to design experimentally tested nanobodies.[[53]] In 2026, Co-Scientist combined hypothesis generation, critique, ranking, and refinement, while Robin connected literature-based reasoning to experimental proposals and analysis in biological research.[[54,55]] These studies support structured collaboration as a useful scientific strategy, but they do not establish that increasing agent count necessarily improves an engineering design. The closer procedural precedents are ACRA, which translates literature procedures into hardware-constrained, executable chemical descriptions, and AutoLabs, which combines user clarification, calculation tools, and self-correction to prepare liquid-handler procedures.[[56,57]] Equipment-aware translation and agent review therefore constitute established foundations on which flow-specific design methods must build.

Scientific-agent research is also shifting toward explicit execution structure and reusable evidence. Paper2Agent converts research papers and their associated code into tested, interactive tools, demonstrating a route beyond retrieval of descriptive passages alone.[[58]] PRISM complements protocol generation with digital-twin feedback to identify physical and sequencing failures before robotic execution.[[62]] The Aspuru-Guzik group's 2026 preprints El Agente Grafico and La Agente Optima address related architectural questions through typed execution state and agent-supervised optimization campaigns, respectively; the latter includes a flow-chemistry demonstration.[[59,60]] These precedents sharpen the scope of the present work. FlowPilot addresses the construction and review of an initial batch-to-flow process proposal, with coupled stream and stage calculations and explicit laboratory constraints. Reaction kinetics and achievable performance remain questions for experimental validation.

Evaluation must reflect that scope. ChemBench showed that strong aggregate chemical-question performance can coexist with elementary errors and overconfident answers.[[61]] Recent work on uncertainty-calibrated experimental optimization also documents invalid or unparsable suggestions from direct LLM prompting within defined reaction search spaces.[[63]] For process design, a plausible narrative, valid output format, and agreement among agents are likewise insufficient: the proposed feeds must satisfy stoichiometry, the stage calculations must share a declared basis, and the assigned equipment must support the stated settings. These requirements motivate a division of responsibility in which LLMs interpret chemistry and propose independent choices, deterministic routines calculate and check dependent quantities, and specialist review examines assumptions and trade-offs. The central question is whether this coordinated workflow improves the consistency and completeness of delivered flow designs relative to one-shot generation under the same chemistry and inventory information.

Here we introduce FlowPilot, a hybrid multi-agent system that connects standardized chemist intake, literature-grounded chemical reasoning, engineering calculation, and inventory-constrained process design. Intake records the protocol, objective, historical evidence, available equipment, and hypotheses as distinct inputs. Chemistry interpretation and plan-aware retrieval supply relevant precedents from a curated flow-chemistry corpus, while handbook-derived rules support the engineering analysis. A seven-agent council reviews and revises candidate designs, after which equipment assignment and numerical reconciliation produce a common final record for the stream tables, stage conditions, and process diagram. This organization makes the relationship between a proposed operating point and its supporting assumptions inspectable. Shorter residence times are candidates to justify and test, not automatic evidence of successful intensification; an inventory-compatible calculation is a proposed experiment, not proof of conversion or safety.

We evaluate this workflow at three distinct levels: information support, computational design quality, and prospective experimental performance. Corpus and rule-base characterization and retrieval tests establish the context supplied to the generator. Matched one-shot and full-pipeline comparisons then use a fixed rubric, deterministic verification evidence, and separate LLM judges across local and commercial models; module ablations and a historical isoxazole model-matrix study examine configuration sensitivity.[[44]] These comparisons assess the delivered workflows rather than isolating agent count or holding total inference cost constant. Finally, photoredox Giese addition followed by aerobic oxidation and telescoped DPDTC-mediated amidation provide complementary two-stage cases for laboratory evaluation.[[45,46]] Together, these evaluations connect the practical task of proposing a first flow experiment to an auditable account of what was retrieved, calculated, reviewed, and ultimately tested.
