FlowPilot addresses batch-to-flow translation as a connected-process design task. Chemistry interpretation, literature retrieval and specialist review determine what should be proposed, while deterministic calculations reconcile dependent quantities and equipment assignments. Their contributions are evaluated at different levels: Figure 3 measures retrieval alignment, Figure 4 compares delivered designs, and the component ablations examine the role of review. A shared process record connects these steps to the stream table and topology, making the relationship between chemical assumptions and engineering conditions explicit.

The matched architecture comparison shows higher mean scores with FlowPilot for each of the five retained generators, with the improvement depending on the model (Figure 4b). In the internal Qwen3.8-27B screen, removing the council reduced the mean score from 0.928 to 0.670 across the three cases (Figure 4d; Table S10). Individual specialist removals had smaller effects. This single-generation-per-cell screen is a descriptive component comparison, not a statistical demonstration that every specialist is necessary. The historical model-matrix and budget experiments further show that broader candidate exploration does not guarantee better outcomes (Figure S12; Tables S18-S21). Together, these results support coordinated review while showing why configuration matters.

This emphasis complements related advances in microreactor design, experimental synthesis development, process simulation and deterministic constraint validation.[[46-51]] FlowPilot links the reaction sequence to explicit feed compositions, molar flows, stage calculations and available equipment in one record. Its focus on coupled stream, stage and equipment consistency also complements process-optimization and materials-development agents.[[60,61]] The comparison in ESI Section 11 describes these differences in task and output; the one-shot benchmark is an architecture comparison within this study, not a head-to-head evaluation of the published systems.

The archived examples show why record-level inspection remains important alongside aggregate scores. Table S26 includes conflicting one-shot residence-time bookkeeping and a FlowPilot catalyst-preparation inconsistency, while Table S22 documents disagreement between structured conditions and an accompanying narrative. These examples motivate a common final record and traceable revision decisions. Retaining the inputs, calculations, equipment selections and council exchanges makes both design conflicts and evaluator errors available for examination rather than hiding them behind a single score.

Figures 5 and 6 connect this computational assessment to two-stage chemical processes. Giese addition followed by oxidation requires control of the oxygen-addition point, irradiation compatibility and gas delivery. DPDTC-mediated amidation requires staged reagent addition, consistent molar feeds and downstream residence-time calculations that account for the increased liquid flow. Their laboratory evaluation will test implementability and final chemical performance separately from numerical closure. The existing feedback interface provides a route to refine subsequent proposals using measured outcomes and actual operating settings. Integration with Bayesian optimization[[30-34]] and language-guided priors[[62]] offers a further direction for testing chemist hypotheses against accumulated evidence. These prospective extensions preserve the same distinction between proposed conditions and observed process behavior.
