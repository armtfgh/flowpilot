[5] ABSTRACT

[6] Designing a continuous-flow synthesis requires chemical judgment about how reaction steps, transport and available equipment should work together. We investigate how language models can support this task through an agentic design workflow, implemented in a newly developed multi-agent pipeline: FlowPilot. Starting from a batch protocol and laboratory constraints, standardized dialogue establishes the design brief. Literature retrieval and a handbook-derived rule base supply documented chemical and engineering knowledge, while a specialist council reviews candidate processes alongside deterministic calculations. A shared design record connects feed compositions, stage conditions, equipment assignments and the process topology. Across three chemistry cases, five generator models and three repeats, a matched architecture comparison produced 90 designs evaluated against 14 fixed criteria by separate LLM judges. Mean benchmark scores increased from 0.794 for one-shot generation to 0.917 for FlowPilot, while judge-reported critical flags decreased from 98 to 4. Laboratory implementation of photoredox Giese addition/oxidation and telescoped DPDTC-mediated amidation gave 97–98% and 68–86% NMR yields, respectively. Different researcher priorities led to distinct stage allocations, but also to convergent selections and experimentally revealed limitations. These results show how an agentic architecture can organize chemical knowledge and quantitative checks around researcher-led flow-process development.

[7] INTRODUCTION

[8] Continuous-flow chemistry allows synthesis to be organized around controlled mixing, heating, irradiation and contact between phases.1-4 These capabilities have broadened access to photochemical, electrochemical and gas–liquid transformations and enabled routes to pharmaceutical intermediates.5-10 Connecting reaction stages can further reduce intermediate handling and support multistep or on-demand production.11-14 For the researcher, however, the opportunity is accompanied by a design problem: choosing an arrangement of streams, reactors and controls that realizes the chemistry with the instruments actually available.15-16 A batch protocol establishes a starting point for this task, but does not specify the continuous process that should be built.

[9] The difficulty lies in the coupling between decisions. Reactor geometry affects irradiation, heat and mass transfer, and pressure loss, while adding a reagent between reactors changes both composition and downstream flow.17-21 Gas delivery couples stoichiometry to phase behavior and makes the reference conditions used for volumetric flow and residence time consequential.22 In a telescoped synthesis, incomplete conversion upstream also changes the material available for the next transformation. Integrated apixaban synthesis and photochemical routes to sartan intermediates illustrate the coordination required across reaction stages and hardware.23-24 An effective design assistant must therefore reason about the connected process, rather than recommend each operating parameter in isolation.

[10] For an experienced flow chemist, designing such a process begins with a feel for the reaction and the apparatus. Whether a feed is likely to remain homogeneous, where a gas should enter, or whether a longer illuminated coil will improve conversion are judgments developed through experiments, troubleshooting and comparison with earlier reactions. This practical intuition is central to choosing which quantities to calculate and which assumptions deserve testing. Flow-development guides and reactor-selection toolboxes make part of that accumulated experience explicit, but their advice acquires meaning only in relation to a particular chemistry and setup.3–4,12,21 A design assistant should support this way of working: bring relevant precedents and engineering principles into the discussion, explain their bearing on a proposed choice, and leave the chemist able to question an analogy or change a priority. Published procedures and handbooks provide a documented part of this experience, without capturing all of the tacit knowledge acquired at the bench.

[11] Digital methods address complementary parts of this challenge. Computer-aided synthesis planning identifies reaction routes,25-27 process simulation evaluates connected unit operations,28-29 and Bayesian or active-learning methods guide experimental optimization.30-34 AI-informed robotic flow synthesis links planning to physical execution,35 while modular platforms such as RoboChem-Flex and LLM-assisted UV-visible auto-reactometry extend automated optimization and screening.36-37 Alongside these developments, researchers need support in formulating the initial process itself: specifying feeds, selecting compatible equipment and defining a defensible operating region before an experimental campaign can refine it.

[12] Large language models (LLMs) offer a way to connect that design intent to specialist tools through natural-language interaction. ChemCrow and Coscientist combine chemical reasoning with tool use and experimentation, whereas El Agente coordinates computational chemistry workflows.38-40 Co-Scientist and Paper2Agent extend collaborative scientific reasoning and the reuse of computational methods.41-42 Closer to laboratory execution, ACRA links literature procedures to executable chemical descriptions and available hardware; AutoLabs combines clarification and self-correction for liquid handling; and PRISM incorporates simulation feedback before robotic execution.43-45 These studies establish the value of agents as coordinators of scientific work, not merely generators of chemical text.

[13] Flow chemistry and process development are already part of this emerging scope. Chat-microreactor uses literature-derived information for flow-pattern prediction and microreactor design guidance,46 and SapoMind combines recommendations with experimental optimization of continuous-flow lanolin saponification.47 LLM-RDF addresses end-to-end synthesis development through agents for literature search, experimental design, execution, analysis and interpretation, including reactor-design tasks.48 Multi-agent process-design systems provide complementary capabilities: Tian et al. generate topologies and parameters for simulation flowsheets,49 and the CeProAgents preprint integrates knowledge extraction, conceptual design and parameter optimization.50 Together, these studies show how chemical agents increasingly connect reactor and process design to broader synthesis-development workflows.

[14] The relevant question is how such capabilities are organized around a laboratory-specific process. The CAAF preprint explores deterministic constraint checking and candidate revision, including a constructed flow-reactor problem,51 while El Agente Gráfico and La Agente Óptima investigate structured scientific execution and agent-supervised optimization.52-53 For researcher-led flow development, this direction calls for a common representation linking the intended chemistry, the quantitative conditions of each stage and the equipment that can realize them. It should also allow the researcher to express priorities, inspect competing designs and return experimental observations without losing the assumptions behind an earlier proposal. The scope and reported validation of directly related systems are compared in Table S1.

[15] Here we investigate how language models can assist the design of connected flow processes when chemical reasoning is coupled to engineering tools and laboratory constraints. We implement this approach in FlowPilot, an agentic research assistant that accepts a batch procedure, available instruments and a design objective. Standardized follow-up questions establish missing requirements, experimental evidence and researcher hypotheses. Retrieval brings relevant published examples into the design context, while a handbook-derived rule base supplies principles and cautions that a flow chemist would consider when evaluating an unfamiliar configuration. The language models interpret this evidence, and a specialist council develops and reviews alternative arrangements of feeds, reactors and controls. Quantitative tools then check whether the proposed conditions and equipment assignments agree. This organization makes the use of documented experience explicit and inspectable while preserving the researcher’s role in experimental decisions.

[16] The central design question is how these capabilities should work together. Chemical interpretation identifies the transformations and their sensitivities; retrieved precedents suggest possible implementations without establishing transferable kinetics; and engineering calculations expose dependencies that may be missed in a fluent description. Council specialists examine these choices jointly before a final configuration is selected. The resulting record connects each stream and reaction stage to the equipment list and diagram, providing a basis for inspection and experimental refinement. Because plausible model reasoning can coexist with elementary errors or overconfidence,54 and model-guided optimization can still propose invalid conditions,55 we assess the delivered process rather than treating the presence of multiple agents as evidence of a better design.

[17] We examine the approach through complementary computational and experimental tests. Retrospective retrieval analyses describe how chemical descriptors influence the selected literature, a matched comparison tests FlowPilot against one-shot generation using the same generator models, and internal ablations examine the contribution of council review. Two laboratory studies then connect researcher priorities and recorded agent reasoning to implemented processes. Photoredox Giese addition followed by oxidation requires a controlled transition from an oxygen-free to an oxygen-fed environment. DPDTC-mediated amidation instead requires coordinated activation, interstage reagent addition and downstream conversion without intermediate isolation. These complementary tests distinguish the quality of a proposed design from its experimental performance and allow both to inform the assessment of agentic process design.

[18] RESULTS

[19] Coordinating chemistry, engineering and laboratory equipment

[20] FlowPilot begins by making the researcher’s process-development problem explicit (Figure 1). A protocol supplies chemical facts, whereas the objective, inventory and follow-up answers define what the proposed process should achieve and what the laboratory can support. The confirmed input package keeps measured evidence separate from hypotheses and missing information. The upstream chemistry module identifies the transformations, stage boundaries, incompatible feeds and sensitivities that shape the design space. Literature retrieval and handbook-derived knowledge then inform an initial quantitative proposal. In this way, a desired chemical outcome is developed into a set of connected operations rather than treated as a request to rewrite the batch procedure.

[21] The council evaluates alternatives where those operations interact (Figure 1d). A Designer proposes candidate configurations; Chemistry, Kinetics, Fluidics and Safety specialists examine transformation fidelity, reaction-time plausibility, transport, hardware and hazards. The Skeptic examines contradictions and unsupported assumptions, and the Orchestrator selects among the reviewed candidates. A shorter residence time, for example, is not considered independently of the reactor volume, achievable feed rate or chemical evidence supporting that exposure. When operating choices change, deterministic calculations update the dependent quantities before the revised candidate is assessed. The review record retains disagreement, rejected alternatives and selection rationales, allowing the researcher to distinguish a constraint-driven decision from an exploratory hypothesis.

[22] Equipment assignment is part of this design process rather than a final list appended to it. Available pump channels, reactor dimensions, thermal limits and compatible irradiation modules constrain which operating points can be realized. The selected proposal is reconciled into a common final record containing the streams, stages, equipment assignments and process graph. Numerical views and the diagram derive from that record; unresolved requirements and earlier calculations remain identifiable as diagnostics. This links what the researcher would assemble to the quantities used to assess it, and preserves a basis for incorporating later experimental observations. Figures S1–S2 and Tables S2–S3 describe the data contracts and provenance mechanisms.

[25] Grounding design choices in literature and engineering knowledge

[26] Candidate design requires both chemical precedents and general engineering knowledge. FlowPilot’s curated literature resource contains 464 classified records spanning thermal synthesis, photoredox catalysis, heterogeneous photocatalysis, cross-coupling, oxidation/reduction and other reaction classes (Figure 2). The recorded hardware includes capillary and coil reactors, microreactors, photoreactors and packed beds, with fluoropolymers prominent among reported tubing materials. Explicit inlet-stream counts are available for 154 processes; two- and three-stream configurations account for 91 and 30 records, respectively. These annotations help relate a chemical plan to alternative ways of supplying reagents and arranging reactors, without prescribing a default topology or treating an absent annotation as evidence that an operation is unnecessary.

[27] The complementary handbook-derived resource contains 2,537 structured rules covering reactor design, heat transfer, catalysts, residence time, photochemistry, materials, scale-up, pressure, mixing and safety (Figure 2e–f). These rules provide engineering context when a close chemical analogue is unavailable or when a candidate must be evaluated beyond reaction identity. Coverage is uneven: heat-transfer rules are densely associated with thermal synthesis and cross-coupling, whereas photochemistry rules concentrate in photoredox and photocycloaddition classes. The rule store informs interpretation and review; only the checks implemented in the calculation layer act as deterministic constraints. Figure S3 complements these coverage summaries by showing field availability and the distributions of reported yields.

[30] Retrieving precedents that reflect the chemistry plan

[31] Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) connects language-model reasoning to external evidence, but its value for flow-process design depends on whether the retrieved literature addresses the chemical problem being solved. An embedding-only semantic search can identify records with similar descriptions while giving insufficient weight to differences in catalyst, reaction environment or operating conditions. FlowPilot therefore extends semantic RAG by using the chemistry plan to guide query construction, filtering and ranking (Figure 3a). Reaction class, mechanism, catalyst identity, solvent and irradiation wavelength make the search chemically explicit. Retrieval initially prioritizes paired batch–flow records under mechanism and phase constraints, preserving access to broader analogies by relaxing these restrictions when close precedents are scarce. A subsequent ranking step combines embedding similarity with agreement in chemical and operating descriptors, directing attention towards records that reflect the intended transformation and its experimental context.

[32] We examined this approach to determine how the added chemical information changes the search description and the evidence prioritized for design. Two illustrative query reconstructions show how a concise reaction description can be expanded to expose variables that would otherwise remain implicit. The number of identified chemical terms increased from 7 to 15 and from 7 to 14 when catalyst, mechanism and operating information were incorporated (Figure 3b). These examples illustrate the information supplied by query enrichment; the term counts themselves do not measure retrieval quality. The relevant question is whether explicitly accounting for such descriptors also changes which precedents are selected.

[33] To examine that effect, we compared embedding-only ranking with a combined semantic and metadata score while holding the stored corpus embeddings fixed. This retrospective analysis evaluated reranking separately from the full live retrieval workflow, using photocatalyst-family agreement as a directly observable measure of alignment. Across 56 queries spanning five photocatalyst families, the mean fraction of the five highest-ranked records sharing the query’s assigned family increased in every group (Figure 3c). The largest changes occurred for iridium complexes, from 40.0% to 95.0%, and organic dyes, from 42.7% to 90.7%; smaller increases were observed for ruthenium complexes, TiO₂ and ZnO. Thus, incorporating catalyst, solvent and wavelength metadata shifted retrieval towards precedents retaining the requested chemical descriptors. Because catalyst-family agreement also contributes to the ranking score, this result demonstrates descriptor alignment rather than independently validating mechanistic relevance.

[34] The resulting change in the evidence presented to the council is illustrated by an iridium-based photoredox search at 465 nm. After reranking, the five highest-ranked records carried recognized iridium-family labels and reported irradiation wavelengths between 425 and 470 nm, making their relationship to the query more explicit (Figure 3d). Interpretation nevertheless depends on metadata quality: an unassigned family can arise from missing information or an unrecognized catalyst name and does not establish a chemical mismatch. Even correctly assigned families encompass catalysts with different ligand environments and redox properties. FlowPilot therefore uses the retrieved records as evidence for further assessment of substrate reactivity, redox requirements and spectral overlap. The contribution of chemistry-aware RAG is to organize that assessment around more closely aligned precedents, while leaving the transfer of conditions to the target reaction subject to council review.

[37] Architecture effects on flow-process design quality

[38] We next asked whether coordinating these capabilities improves the delivered process design beyond what the same LLM can produce in one response (Figure 4a). The three benchmark chemistries were selected to expose complementary process-design demands while using one unchanged assessment rubric. Cu/C-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) tests whether catalyst strategy, liquid-feed preparation and heated, pressurized operation remain consistent when a batch procedure is converted into a proposed apparatus. Oxidation of Fmoc-L-methionine tests the coordination of photocatalyst, irradiation and oxygen delivery, including the distinction between total air flow and reactive oxygen equivalents. Hydrogenolysis of N-diphenylmethylazetidin-3-ol adds a gas–liquid–solid contact problem, for which catalyst-bed placement, pressure control and the definition of contact time must agree. These contrasts challenge different assumptions even when a model correctly recognizes the transformation.

[39] Selected after pilot development, the cases act as a retrospective stress test of connected design, rather than a representative survey of synthetic chemistry or a competition to predict published yields. For each case, one-shot generation and FlowPilot received the same protocol, objective, authoritative inventory and requested final-output structure. The one-shot baseline received the complete design brief, not merely a short chemistry question. Applicable criteria were selected by fixed rules rather than adjusted to favor a chemistry or architecture; source records were excluded from FlowPilot retrieval, although this does not establish their absence from model pretraining. ESI Section 4 retains the original prompts, source references and case-specific inventory limits, including ambiguities in the frozen inputs.

[40] The six-model comparison included Qwen3.6-27B and Qwen3.8-27B alongside GPT-4o and GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Claude Opus 4.6. This spans locally served 27B models and commercial model families, allowing the architecture effect to be examined separately from choosing a particular generator. Each model was tested in both architectures for three repeats of each case, giving 90 outcomes. Three separate Qwen, OpenAI and Claude evaluator calls assessed each normalized final design using the same 14 criteria, covering chemical fidelity, feed chemistry, numerical and topology consistency, inventory, physical plausibility, operating procedures and uncertainty. Applicable integer ratings from 0 to 4 were averaged with equal judge and criterion weights and divided by four; critical flags were retained separately. Tables S4–S5 define the rubric, and Table S6 specifies the complementary module-ablation screen.

[41] The comparison holds task information constant, not computation: FlowPilot can retrieve evidence, call tools and revise candidates, whereas one-shot generation uses one model call. It therefore tests the utility of the complete design architecture, not whether agent dialogue alone causes an improvement at equal token cost. For each model and architecture, the three case scores were averaged within each repeat; the reported mean and sample standard deviation summarize those three repeat-level values. Generator and architecture labels were removed from evaluator packets, but judging remained an LLM-based assessment, with shared model families and potentially recognizable output styles. The exact inputs, evaluator configuration, applicability rules and repeat structure are retained in ESI Section 4 and Methods.

[44] FlowPilot increased the mean score for each of the five generators (Figure 4b). Across this cohort, the architecture means were 0.917 for FlowPilot and 0.794 for one-shot generation. The gain was largest for GPT-4o (+0.226), followed by Qwen3.8-27B (+0.168) and Qwen3.6-27B (+0.139); Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.6 showed smaller gains of +0.034 and +0.049. The one-shot means spanned 0.684–0.890, whereas FlowPilot means occupied the narrower range of 0.904–0.932. This pattern suggests that the design architecture was particularly useful when direct generation left more requirements unresolved. The two 27B models within FlowPilot also exceeded the commercial one-shot means in this cohort, supporting the practical value of workflow structure without establishing that a smaller model is intrinsically more capable. The smaller Claude gains are equally informative: stronger direct outputs leave less room for improvement. Figure 4 additionally retains an archived GPT-5.4 comparison, which is not pooled into the five-model summaries.

[45] The critical-flag analysis asks whether the higher average scores correspond to fewer consequential defects in the proposed process (Figure 4c). Judges recorded 98 judge-level critical flags across 45 one-shot outcomes and 4 across 45 FlowPilot outcomes; 18 and 43 outcomes, respectively, had no flag. Panel c groups these findings by generator: each point is the mean total across the three chemistries within a repeat, not a measured failure frequency. The large reductions for the Qwen models and GPT-4o are consistent with the larger score gains in panel b, whereas the Claude one-shot designs already contained fewer flagged defects. This makes the reduction more informative than a score increase alone, but does not convert the assessment into a prediction of laboratory success.

[46] The underlying examples illustrate why these defects matter chemically and operationally. A one-shot hydrogenolysis procedure placed a vented gas–liquid separator before its only pressure-defining BPR while describing pressurized reaction, leaving the proposed gas contact and pressure boundary incompatible as written. Another mixed an empty-bed time with a different reported residence-time value, obscuring the exposure assigned to the substrate. FlowPilot was not defect-free: one CuAAC output placed Cu/C in the liquid preparation despite specifying a packed catalyst bed, a contradiction that changes how the catalyst is contacted and retained. Moreover, one judge incorrectly treated equal-reference sccm and mL min−1 values as differing by a factor of 1,000. The archived counts retain that false positive, and multiple flags can refer to one underlying defect. They are therefore reported as judge findings, not as independently confirmed physical errors or safety probabilities.

[47] The case-specific analysis helps locate the improvement (Figure S4). Hydrogenolysis showed the largest mean gain for both Qwen models and for Opus 4.6, consistent with the demands of coordinating gas delivery, catalyst contact and pressure control. At criterion level, the displayed gains in executable topology were positive for every model–case mean, whereas transformation-fidelity changes were small in comparison (Figure S5). This pattern points to process integration, not simply recognition of the named reaction, as an important source of the observed benefit. Not every criterion improved, and the run-level flag map retains the remaining failures (Figure S6). Resource use must also be considered: Figure S7 reports tokens, runtime and generation cost, while Figure 4e relates score to cost under the archived price schedule. The Qwen configurations offered higher score-per-generation-cost ratios under that schedule; this is a deployment-relevant trade-off, not a measurement of local hardware costs or experimental productivity. Table S7 defines the numerical closure checks underlying the engineering assessment.

[48] To examine the role of coordinated review, we separately tested 15 Qwen3.8-27B configurations over the same three cases. Figure 4d and Table S6 show the seven conditions that compare the full pipeline with one-shot generation, removal of the council and removal of individual domain reviewers; the other eight conditions remain in the archived screen. Removing the council reduced the mean score from 0.928 to 0.670, below the 0.743 one-shot result. The initial sequence of agent calls without council review therefore did not recover the quality of the complete configuration. This is consistent with a benefit from reviewing whether chemical and engineering choices form a coherent process, although one generation per case cannot isolate that mechanism from generation variability.

[49] The domain removals help interpret that result. Without the Chemistry reviewer, the mean was 0.902; without Fluidics, Safety or Kinetics it was 0.913, 0.915 or 0.924, respectively. These reviewers examine coupled decisions such as whether a supported catalyst remains in the intended phase, whether gas and liquid feeds support the stated contact time, and whether thermal and pressure assumptions are compatible with the solvent and hardware. Their overlapping responsibilities allow another reviewer or a deterministic check to catch some omissions, so the smaller changes do not establish that a removed specialty is unimportant. Each condition was generated once per chemistry; the bars show between-case variation, not replicate uncertainty, and the screen cannot resolve a causal ranking of individual agents.

[50] The interface changes how that design brief is assembled and how the output is inspected. A worked Claude Opus 4.6 example separates researcher-supplied protocol text from software-assembled context (Figure S8) and compares the returned process diagram with the direct model response (Figure S9). It illustrates an interaction and artifact-management difference, not a token-saving claim: FlowPilot used more internal computation. The historical heuristic QA values displayed in this example are distinct from the fixed-criteria benchmark scores reported above.

[51] These assessments also need to remain useful to the researcher making an experimental decision. The interface retains the original protocol and answered follow-up questions (Figure S10), and the categorized inventory and constraints (Figure S11; Table S8). The audit examples include pressure-control and residence-time inconsistencies in one-shot designs, a catalyst-placement defect in a FlowPilot output, and an evaluator false positive (Table S9). This connection between design, evidence and review provides the starting point for testing the proposed process in the laboratory.

[52] Connected design extends beyond reaction conditions to subsequent material handling, as illustrated by continuous isoxazole synthesis coupled to inline solvent separation and recovery.56 An earlier FlowPilot computational dry run examined aldehyde α-bromination followed by an inline quench, connecting a reaction step to downstream reagent deactivation (ESI Section 6.4; Figure S12). This archived illustration is retained as a pre-experimental design example, including its unresolved mixing warnings, rather than as an additional laboratory validation. The two studies below instead examine actual implementation of connected reaction sequences.

[53] We next examined how agentic design decisions carry through to the laboratory, using two connected-stage reactions that were separate from the architecture-benchmark cases. For each chemistry, the batch protocol and available inventory were held constant while three standardized response sets varied the researcher’s objective and hypotheses (Figures 5b and 6b). Set 1 emphasized final yield without unnecessarily long holds, Set 2 emphasized high conversion with justified starting times, and Set 3 emphasized a balance between yield and throughput. These related priorities test how the workflow responds to the researcher’s intent; they are not three different reactions or experimental replicates. Each run considered 12 candidates. Some selections converged, leaving two distinct implemented operating points per chemistry. Tables S10–S11 provide the responses, Figures S13–S18 retain the selected topologies, and ESI Section 7.3 gives the detailed council reports. The condensed statements in the main figures summarize these reports rather than reproduce a literal dialogue.

[54] Staged oxygen delivery in photoredox Giese addition and oxidation

[55] The first case links photoredox carbon–carbon bond formation to oxidation of the resulting sulfide (Figure 5a). An alpha-thiomethyl radical generated from alpha-silyl sulfide 1a adds to acrylonitrile 2a; subsequent oxidation affords sulfoxide 4a.57 In batch, the chemistry is separated in time: 4 h under argon precedes a further 6 h of irradiation after exposure to air. The process-design challenge is to preserve this sequence in space. FlowPilot therefore had to maintain an oxygen-free first photoreactor and introduce the oxidant only before the downstream oxidation stage. The confirmed brief specified at least two oxygen equivalents, making the location and molar delivery of gas part of the chemical design rather than an ancillary equipment choice.

[56] The researcher–council exchange in Figure 5b shows how that requirement shaped candidate review. Chemistry preserved the order of transformations; Kinetics compared the two stage exposures without predicting yield; and Fluidics and Safety examined the gas-injection junction and reverse-flow pathway. The Skeptic distinguished numerical consistency from evidence of conversion or safe operation. Within these shared constraints, the Chief selected a 2 mL first-stage coil for Sets 1/3 and a 5 mL coil for the conversion-focused Set 2. Both retained the same liquid feed and 20 mL oxidation coil. Thus the response to a stronger conversion priority was additional upstream exposure, whereas the smaller coil was a more compact starting point, not a higher-throughput one. This convergence also exposes a limit of the objective interpretation: the recorded policy treated Sets 1 and 3 as balanced rather than as distinct yield and throughput modes (ESI Section 7.3).

[57] The proposed arrangement and its apparatus illustration are shown separately in Figure 5c–d. We implemented a 2 or 5 mL PFA coil in a Vapourtec UV-150 module followed by a separately illuminated 20 mL FEP coil, both at 25 ± 1 °C. A 2.0 mL sample loop introduced 1a (0.100 M), acrylonitrile (0.200 M) and iridium photocatalyst (0.500 mM) into a carrier stream at 0.020 mL min−1. The nominal first-stage liquid times were consequently 100 and 250 min. Pure oxygen entered at the interstage mixer at 0.090 mL min−1 on the proposal’s inlet/STP basis, corresponding to approximately 2.01 equivalents relative to the nominal substrate feed. The calculated second-stage index, 20/(0.020 + 0.090) = 181.82 min, uses gas volume at STP and must not be read as residence time under reactor pressure. ESI Section 8 reports the implemented procedure and distinguishes these nominal calculations from the gas-controller reference state and collection details still requiring confirmation.

[58] Experimental feedback revealed why gas stoichiometry alone was insufficient to establish a workable arrangement. An earlier trial using pure oxygen at 0.43 mL min−1 with liquid at 0.020 mL min−1 produced backflow toward the first reactor. Pure oxygen was then specified by the researcher for the revised designs at the lower setting of 0.090 mL min−1; this change was not an autonomous model discovery. Council review retained the unresolved upstream reverse-flow pathway because a check valve on the gas branch does not protect the liquid branch. At the subsequently implemented conditions, grouped Sets 1/3 gave 97% NMR yield and 94% isolated yield, while Set 2 gave 98% NMR yield (Figure 5e). The literature flow reference gave 90% NMR and 88% isolated yield under different conditions; the literature batch value of 95% is an isolated yield.57 The similar NMR yields at 100 and 250 min in R1 do not establish a benefit from the longer upstream hold without replicate measurements. Rather, the shorter configuration provides a supported starting point for further investigation. Figures S19–S21 retain the irradiation specifications, original experimental scheme and condition table, and apparatus photograph. The case connects explicit agent reasoning with a successful chemical outcome while showing that hydraulic uncertainty must remain visible after selection.

[61] Coupled feed and residence-time design for telescoped amidation

[62] The second case examines whether an agentic design can balance formation and consumption of a reactive intermediate across a telescoped sequence (Figure 6a). DPDTC and DMAP convert 3-methyl-4-nitrobenzoic acid 1b to a 2-pyridyl thioester, which undergoes aminolysis with benzylamine 2b to form amide 3b.58 The batch procedure uses two 30 min periods at 95 °C, separated by cooling and amine addition. FlowPilot preserved activation before aminolysis while considering direct hot transfer in place of batch handling. This is a coupled chemical and engineering choice: the composition leaving R1 governs the second reaction, and the added amine feed changes both stoichiometry and downstream exposure.

[63] The council comparison in Figure 6b made that coupling explicit. Chemistry established the interstage addition, Kinetics compared near-batch holds with longer activation and shorter aminolysis, and Fluidics checked concentrations, molar ratios and cumulative downstream flow. Safety reviewed the heated, pressurized configuration and feed compatibility, while the Skeptic retained uncertainty about Stage 2 conversion. The Chief selected two 5 mL coils for Set 1. Sets 2/3 converged on a 10 mL activation coil followed by a 5 mL amidation coil at higher feed rates (Figure 6c). The latter increased upstream exposure while shortening the downstream hold. The recorded rationale therefore identified an experimental trade-off rather than predicting that a higher feed rate would preserve yield; the full reports are retained in ESI Section 7.3.

[64] Implementation used two channels of the same Vapourtec E-series system, direct interstage mixing and no intermediate isolation (Figure 6d). Feed A contained acid (0.500 M), DPDTC (0.525 M) and DMAP (0.0500 M) in 2-MeTHF; Feed B contained benzylamine (2.10 M). Set 1 used rates of 0.160 and 0.0400 mL min−1, delivering nominal acid and amine flows of 0.0800 and 0.0840 mmol min−1, respectively. These concentration–flow products preserve 1.05 equivalents of amine. Two 5 mL ETFE coils gave times of 31.25 and 25.00 min, with the latter calculated from the combined liquid flow. Both coils operated at 95 °C. Feed A was introduced through a 2.0 mL sample loop, and recorded system pressure was 5.2–5.3 bar with a 7 bar cartridge BPR. The distinction between stock concentration, molar delivery and local reactor flow is essential: two independently specified stage times would not, by themselves, define a consistent telescoped process.

[65] Set 1 afforded 86% NMR yield and 84% isolated yield (Figure 6e). The second operating point, shared by Sets 2/3, used feed rates of 0.280 and 0.0700 mL min−1 with 10 and 5 mL coils, giving stage times of 35.71 and 14.29 min and 68% NMR yield. Nominal acid delivery increased by 75%, from 0.0800 to 0.140 mmol min−1, while total nominal stage time decreased from 56.25 to 50.00 min. The lower yield despite a longer activation hold shows why the complete sequence, rather than R1 alone, must guide refinement. It is consistent with the council’s concern about shortened downstream exposure, but does not identify the rate-limiting step because reactor volume and flow changed together and intermediate conversion was not measured. The literature values of 98% NMR yield in batch and 97% isolated yield in flow are contextual references, not matched controls.58 Figures S22–S23 and ESI Section 8 retain the original experimental scheme, condition table, apparatus and preparation details. For subsequent agent-guided development, these measured outcomes provide a reason to revisit stage allocation while preserving the verified feed stoichiometry. The finite sample-loop experiments do not establish sustained production rates.

[68] DISCUSSION

[69] This study examines how language models can contribute to flow-process design when their reasoning is organized around the connected experiment. FlowPilot provides a concrete implementation: a researcher begins with a procedure and available instruments, expresses a priority, and inspects configurations that connect chemical rationale to quantitative conditions and equipment. The approach draws on the way flow chemists use precedents, engineering principles and practical constraints together, while exposing those connections for review. It complements microreactor assistants, synthesis-development platforms and process-simulation agents by focusing their related capabilities on a laboratory-specific design task.46–51,59–60 The matched comparison then asks what this organization adds beyond direct generation by the same model.

[70] The benchmark helps explain why this organization matters. The same generator produced higher mean scores within FlowPilot for all five models, and improvements extended to topology, material balance and inventory rather than being confined to chemical nomenclature. In the module screen, simply separating the initial workflow into stages without council review performed poorly, whereas coordinated review recovered much of the design quality. At the same time, simpler council settings occasionally matched or exceeded the complete configuration. These findings argue for selective, accountable review of coupled decisions rather than maximizing agent count or discussion length. They also identify a practical opportunity for locally served models: the tested 27B generators delivered competitive assessed designs when supported by the architecture, although additional computation is part of that benefit.

[71] The laboratory studies show both the value and the limits of making these decisions explicit. In Giese addition/oxidation, the council preserved spatial separation of the two reaction environments and selected different upstream exposures in response to the brief. The similar observed yields did not establish an advantage for the longer coil, and the earlier backflow incident exposed a pressure-interaction problem that remained unresolved by stoichiometric closure. In amidation, the higher-feed-rate selection shortened downstream exposure and gave lower yield despite a longer activation hold. These results turn the council’s stated uncertainties into experimentally grounded questions for refinement. They also show why different wording need not produce different apparatus: the response sets converged on two operating points per chemistry. The experiments establish product formation under those conditions, rather than a controlled ranking of three objectives or proof of sustained production.

[72] A useful design assistant must consequently retain both the rationale for a proposal and the evidence that later challenges it. FlowPilot’s saved inputs, stage calculations, equipment assignments and council records support this relationship between researcher and model, while the remaining design defects and evaluator false positive show why neither model confidence nor a benchmark score should substitute for scientific judgment. Experimental observations can be returned with the conditions actually used, so that subsequent proposals respond to measured process behavior rather than repeated assumptions. Coupling this record to Bayesian optimization or language-guided experimental priors offers a route to more systematic prospective refinement.30-34,61 The next test of that workflow is whether it reduces experimental effort across additional chemistries and inventories, not merely whether it produces a more persuasive initial answer.

[73] CONCLUSION

[74] Language models can assist flow-process development when chemical reasoning, quantitative engineering and equipment constraints are brought into a common design workflow. FlowPilot implements this approach through standardized researcher input, literature and handbook knowledge, and council review of connected candidate processes. Across the five-model architecture comparison, mean benchmark scores increased from 0.794 for one-shot generation to 0.917 for FlowPilot, with fewer judge-reported critical flags. The internal screen supports a contribution from coordinated review without establishing that more agents or longer deliberation always improve a design. Laboratory implementation demonstrated the Giese addition/oxidation and amidation sequences at 97–98% and 68–86% NMR yield, respectively. Their contrasting outcomes show how a recorded rationale can connect an initial proposal to the experimental questions that follow: whether additional exposure is useful, whether a downstream hold is sufficient, and whether the assembled pressure boundary behaves as assumed. FlowPilot makes these decisions available for scrutiny and revision. The broader contribution is a way to study and use agentic reasoning in process design while keeping chemical judgment and experimental evidence central.

[75] METHODS

[76] Standardized inputs and study organization

[77] The standardized workflow begins with a batch protocol and a structured intake package recording the objective, chemistry identity, experimental history, laboratory inventory, operating limits, and chemist hypotheses. Deterministic protocol triggers activate questions from a versioned bank of stable identifiers, and confirmed package state determines which remain pending. The LLM extracts and interprets answers but cannot introduce new identifiers or override readiness. Information such as historical evidence can be explicitly marked unavailable; conditional gas, irradiation and stage questions follow the resolution rules in Table S3. The confirmed package is frozen for the run, retaining the answers and their provenance. Measured observations, protocol facts, and hypotheses are stored separately. Hard safety and equipment constraints remain mandatory even when earlier measurements were obtained under different conditions. Computational comparisons used archived case packages rather than newly generated conversations, keeping the supplied chemistry and constraints constant between architectures. The intake contract and question rules are given in ESI Section 2 and Tables S2–S3. Benchmark campaigns and subsequent laboratory-design runs were treated as distinct software/configuration records; the dated development history is summarized in ESI Section 9 and Table S12.

[78] Literature corpus and plan-aware retrieval

[79] The literature resource comprised 464 classified flow-chemistry records annotated with available reaction, reactor, material, operating-condition, yield and inlet-stream information. Missing metadata were retained as missing rather than filled from model inference. A complementary handbook-derived store contained 2,537 engineering rules with topic, severity, source and applicability information.3–4,21 Relevant rules supplied context to chemistry interpretation; only checks implemented in the calculation and validation routines acted as deterministic constraints. Corpus field availability is reported in Figure S3; rule-base coverage is summarized in Figure 2e–f.

[80] For operational retrieval, the chemistry plan supplies reaction class, mechanism, catalyst, solvent, phase regime, and operating conditions. The search begins with paired batch-flow records under mechanism and phase filters, relaxes filters when fewer than three candidates remain, and expands to the full corpus when no paired result remains. The final score combines semantic and field similarity with weights of 0.60 and 0.40. In the architecture benchmark, the designated source record was excluded before final retrieval selection; this restriction does not establish absence from model pretraining.

[81] The retrospective figure analyses used stored corpus embeddings and inverse Euclidean-distance similarity, 1/(1 + d), rather than newly generated query embeddings. The rank analysis reranked the 20 nearest non-self records for 80 queries using photocatalyst, solvent, and wavelength terms weighted 0.30, 0.20, and 0.20; temperature and concentration were not included in that offline score. A separate family analysis sampled up to 15 queries per recorded family and measured the fraction of matching-family records in each top-five result set. These analyses measure metadata alignment, not the full live retrieval policy or independent chemical correctness.

[82] Engineering calculations and stage accounting

[83] The engineering layer recalculated quantities dependent on proposed operating choices. Component molar delivery was obtained from concentration and liquid volumetric flow, and each stage received the cumulative flow of the streams introduced at or upstream of that stage. For a nominal liquid-filled reactor, the stage time was calculated as reactor volume divided by local liquid flow. An interstage feed therefore changed the downstream material balance and time calculation without changing the upstream flow. Packed-bed empty volume, estimated liquid holdup, and catalyst-contact time were distinguished where applicable. Stage totals were formed only from times reported on compatible bases.

[84] Gas equivalents were calculated from the reactive-gas molar flow divided by the limiting-reactant molar flow, including gas composition and the stated reference temperature and pressure. The proposal STP convention was 273.15 K and 1 atm. Where required by a benchmark contract, in-channel gas volume was derived by ideal-gas scaling using absolute operating pressure and temperature. The inlet-reference index V/(Qliq + Qgas,STP) was kept separate from pressure-corrected nominal time and from measured residence time. Ideal-gas correction alone does not determine liquid holdup or residence-time distribution; a no-slip estimate additionally neglects phase slip, gas dissolution or consumption, and axial pressure variation. Pressure-loss and thermal screening used the selected geometry, phase assumptions, and available material properties; estimated heat-transfer or reaction-enthalpy inputs were identified as assumptions. The implemented quantities, multiphase conventions, and heat-transfer screening are detailed in ESI Section 5 and Table S7.

[85] Candidate generation and council review

[86] The downstream Designer generated alternative geometries and operating choices from the chemistry plan, retrieved analogies, engineering calculations, and inventory constraints. The Chemistry, Kinetics, Fluidics, and Safety specialists reviewed chemical compatibility, reaction-time plausibility, transport and hardware feasibility, and hazards, respectively. The Skeptic examined assumptions and contradictions, and the Orchestrator selected among the reviewed candidates. Agent verdicts and proposed revisions were stored with candidate identifiers. Changes to independent variables triggered deterministic recalculation before a candidate could be compared or published. Candidate and revision budgets followed the configuration recorded for each campaign rather than being inferred from the final output. Rejected alternatives and revision history were retained alongside the selected candidate. The component responsibilities are specified in ESI Section 2.1, the ablation configurations in Table S6, and the experimental-case council records in ESI Section 7.3.

[87] Inventory realization and result publication

[88] Inventory profiles were entered through categorized equipment forms, imported as structured JSON, or normalized from laboratory documents into typed records with stable identifiers. Available quantities, system membership, material, reactor volume and diameter, pump range and setting increment, pressure and temperature limits, and compatible light modules were recorded where supplied. The form fields share the pipeline's equipment schema, and edited profiles require validation before export or design use. Realization assigned required operations to available items and checked the declared capability and connectivity constraints. Calculations were repeated when equipment selection changed a dependent quantity. An unresolved essential requirement was retained as a diagnostic requirement, not silently replaced with invented hardware (Figure S11; Table S8).

[89] The final design contract reconciled the selected proposal, stage table, stream table, equipment allocation, and topology. GUI numerical views and the executable process diagram were derived from that record; earlier calculations and council exchanges remained separately available for inspection. Timestamped run folders stored the input package, result, final contract, topology, equipment allocation, and available diagram artifacts. Experimental feedback was linked to the conditions actually tested before being supplied to a later design cycle. ESI Sections 2.4–2.5 and 6 document the GUI and saved artifacts, with the machine-readable feedback and provenance records retained in the companion archive. Exact computational replay additionally requires the model, decoding settings, dependencies, and software revision recorded for the campaign.

[90] Matched architecture comparison and LLM judging

[91] The primary comparison used CuAAC, photochemical oxidation, and hydrogenolysis case packages with the same protocol, objective, inventory, and final-output contract for both architectures. One-shot generation received this information in a single call; FlowPilot distributed it across its interpretation, calculation, review, and realization stages. Qwen3.6-27B, Qwen3.8-27B, GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and Claude Opus 4.6 were each used with both architectures for three generation repeats per case, giving 90 outcomes at the recorded generation temperature of 0.2. The archived GPT-5.4 generator results additionally displayed in Figure 4b–c were not included in these five-model aggregates. ESI Section 4 describes the case prompts, inventory limits and model records; the accompanying frozen-input files retain the exact JSON and complete design-input strings. The comparison concerns delivered design quality under matched task information, not equal token or computation budgets.

[92] For judging, final designs were normalized into candidate packets with generator and architecture labels removed. Separate Qwen, OpenAI, and Claude judge calls received the candidate, common input context, reference facts, deterministic verification evidence, and the fixed 14-criterion rubric. The archived judge configuration used Qwen3.6-27B, GPT-5.4, and Claude Sonnet 4.6 at temperature 0.0; these evaluator calls were separate from candidate generation. Each applicable criterion received an integer rating from 0 to 4. Ratings were averaged across judges for each criterion, then across applicable criteria, and divided by four to give a score from 0 to 1. All judges and applicable criteria had equal weight. Gas bookkeeping and multistage closure could be excluded only when their applicability conditions were false; missing required information was scored rather than treated as inapplicable. Critical flags were counted separately from the numerical score. Deterministic evidence did not confer an architecture-specific bonus, and identity masking did not guarantee that output style concealed the architecture. Some evaluator model families also served as generators; separate calls therefore do not establish statistical independence or remove possible model-family preferences. Scoring anchors, criterion definitions, and applicability rules are specified in Tables S4–S5.

[93] Ablation experiments and statistical reporting

[94] For each generator and architecture, the three case scores were averaged within each repeat. Reported architecture error bars are the sample standard deviation of these three repeat-level means, not the dispersion of all nine individual outcomes. Critical-flag counts were summed across the three cases within a repeat before calculating their mean and sample standard deviation. The aggregate values reported here use only the five-model cohort. Generation cost was estimated from recorded token usage and the archived model-specific price schedule, excluding evaluator calls and deployment costs. Figure 4e uses mean score divided by mean generation cost; the case-specific ESI plots instead summarize per-outcome score-to-cost ratios (Figure S7). The comparisons are descriptive; no statistical-significance claim or population-wide generalization is made from three repeats and three selected chemistries.

[95] The internal ablation screen fixed the generator to Qwen3.8-27B and tested 15 conditions over the same three cases, producing 45 outcomes with one generation per condition-case pair. Figure 4d and Table S6 report the seven conditions focused on council and domain-reviewer removal; all 15 conditions remain in the archived module summary. Disabled specialists received no substitute score, and active council weights were renormalized. Standard deviations describe between-case variation, not repeatability. No experimental yield uncertainty was inferred from these computational repeats.

[96] Laboratory implementation and chemical analysis

[97] We implemented the two connected-stage cases using a Vapourtec E-series system with sample-loop injection. For the Giese addition/oxidation sequence, a PFA coil in a 450 nm UV-150 module was followed by a separately illuminated FEP coil. The first stage was designed to remain oxygen-free, although the exact feed-degassing procedure was not specified in the supplied laboratory methods. Pure oxygen was introduced through an MFC at the interstage mixer, and both reactors were maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. For DPDTC-mediated amidation, the acid/DPDTC/DMAP solution and benzylamine solution were delivered by separate channels, with amine addition between two ETFE coils maintained at 95 °C. Reported concentrations and molar feeds describe the nominal sample solution, not a measured concentration profile throughout the dispersed injection. Both setups used a 7 bar cartridge BPR; recorded system pressure was reported separately from the cartridge rating. Actual concentrations, molar feeds, operating points, and proposal-to-implementation differences are tabulated in ESI Section 8, Figure S20 and Figure S22.

[98] NMR yields were reported using 1,3-benzodioxole as the internal standard. NMR and isolated yields are distinguished in Figures 5e and 6e; an unreported value was not inferred from the other yield measure. Isolated products were characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy and low-resolution mass spectrometry. Shared response-set entries were treated as grouped operating points, not independent replicates, and no experimental standard deviation was assigned without individual measurements. ESI Section 8 contains feed preparation, work-up, irradiation specifications, apparatus photographs and characterization records; Figures S24–S27 reproduce the product spectra. Gas-controller reference conditions, collection windows and quantitative-NMR calculation records remain identified as outstanding metadata.
